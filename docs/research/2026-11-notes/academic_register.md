# Academic-register tells of LLM-written and LLM-assisted prose

Scope note. Baseline coverage read before research: `serious-academic` SKILL.md (twelve commandments: reader context, one point per paragraph, sentence connection, problem before mechanism, no unasked rebuttals, term discipline, no change history, selection, one idea per sentence, numbers explained, concrete case first, claims sized to evidence with limits stated once; plus "never invent a citation" and "check cited claims against the source") and `no-ai-tells` 0.9.0 (banned words incl. commendable, meticulous, intricate, notable, pivotal, innovative, groundbreaking, paradigm, comprehensive, robust; phrases incl. "plays a crucial role", "valuable insights", "has become increasingly important", "In conclusion"; leakage incl. "as an AI" and knowledge-cutoff disclaimers; structures incl. rule of three, participial tail, significance inflation, vague attribution, header/bullet inflation, recap close). Each finding below is tagged with evidence strength: STRONG (large corpus or controlled study with quantified effect), MODERATE (small or single-domain quantified study), WEAK (unreviewed preprint of unclear provenance, vendor blog, or anecdote). Each "Inferences" block ends with a COVERAGE line saying what the two skills already handle and what is new. Detection tests and rewrite rules are inferences drawn from the cited findings, not claims made by the sources.

## 1. Metadiscourse: how LLM academic text differs in hedges, boosters, attitude, engagement, self-mention, bundles

### Takeaway
Default LLM academic text is well organized (plenty of interactive, signposting metadiscourse) but thin on interactional metadiscourse: fewer hedges, fewer attitude markers, fewer self-mentions, weaker authorial presence, and a narrower, more formulaic set of lexical bundles. The deficit is prompt-sensitive: prompting for stance closes the hedge gap but makes boosters overshoot human levels, so "add hedges" is the wrong fix.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Jiang and Hyland (2025, English for Specific Purposes 79:17-29): ChatGPT argumentative essays, "though structurally coherent and logically organised, exhibit a significantly lower frequency of interactional metadiscourse, such as hedges, boosters, and attitude markers, leading to a more impersonal and expository tone", while student essays use "nuanced stance markers and personalised expressions". [UEA repository](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/99123/)
- STRONG. Jiang and Hyland (2025, Journal of English for Academic Purposes 62, 101273), "Metadiscourse in AI-generated versus student academic writing", reports the same direction (cited in the Shiraz study's reference list and summary). [Shiraz TESL article](https://tesl.shirazu.ac.ir/article_8833_bd7da341b6c638e9f62814a8620ce174.pdf)
- STRONG. Jiang and Hyland (2025, Applied Linguistics 46(3):375-391), 3-word bundles: ChatGPT essays "contain a lower frequency of bundles but these have a higher type/token ratio, suggesting that its bundles are more rigid and formulaic"; noun- and preposition-based bundles dominate, "employed for abstract descriptions and to provide transitional and structuring cues"; student essays have "more epistemic stances and authorial presence". [Applied Linguistics](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/46/3/375/7736875)
- STRONG. Jiang and Hyland (2026, Applied Linguistics Review), stance: AI texts "contained significantly fewer stance expressions, deployed a narrower lexical repertoire, and projected markedly weaker authorial presence than the student essays". [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/applirev-2025-0188/html)
- MODERATE. Hyland (2025, RELC Journal) summarizes the program: ChatGPT uses "a narrower selection and more repetitive range of lexical bundles, significantly fewer epistemic and attitudinal stance markers, particularly questions and personal asides, and exhibits far less authorial presence". [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00336882251357675)
- STRONG, prompt-sensitivity. Crosthwaite et al. (2026, Modern Language Journal): with a baseline essay prompt, GPT texts had significantly fewer interactive, interactional and engagement features than LOCNESS student essays (p < 0.0001), and specifically fewer hedges (p < 0.001), self-mentions (p < 0.0001) and attitude markers (p < 0.01), with boosters similar. A "metadiscourse-inducing" prompt brought hedges and attitude markers to non-significant difference from humans, but "the use of boosters (previously non-significant) has now significantly increased"; the authors conclude patterns are "highly prompt-sensitive rather than intrinsically human-like or human-unlike". [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/modl.70089); slides with effect sizes (humans use more hedges U=55.83, p=0.012; boosters U=51.91, p<.001, rank e2=0.185) at [Edge Hill CRG slides](https://sites.edgehill.ac.uk/crg/wp-content/uploads/sites/377/2026/03/EHU-CRG.20.Crosthwaite.Slides.pdf)
- MODERATE, research-article genre (closest to papers). Shiraz study of discussion and conclusion sections in 40 applied linguistics research articles (human vs GPT-4): human texts used significantly more hedges (30.02 vs 21.04 per 1,000 words), engagement markers (7.70 vs 4.47 per 1,000) and attitude markers; boosters similar (19.68 vs 17.87); AI texts "overused self-mentions". [Shiraz TESL](https://tesl.shirazu.ac.ir/article_8833_bd7da341b6c638e9f62814a8620ce174.pdf)
- MODERATE, conflicting detail. A comparison of 100 human vs 100 ChatGPT linguistics research articles found hedges were a larger share of all interactional markers in ChatGPT text (68.52% vs 54.58%) and engagement markers slightly denser in ChatGPT text per 10,000 words (3.85 vs 3.15), while also stating ChatGPT "tend[s] to underuse hedges, boosters, self-mentions, and engagement markers" overall. [UPI IJAL](https://vm113.upi.edu/index.php/ijal/article/download/101/55)
- WEAK (unattributed PDF, venue unknown). Abstract study: AI abstracts had more interactive markers (transitions, frame signals) and hedges at about half the human rate (chi-square p < 0.001); first-person "we"/"our findings" "near-absent in ChatGPT abstracts", which favored passives. [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/579d/f4c7c629c0df17e1846558b97f2f7636b4c1.pdf)

### Inferences
- Self-mention direction conflicts: student-essay studies (Jiang and Hyland; Crosthwaite) find fewer self-mentions in AI text, the Shiraz research-article study finds more. A plausible reconciliation (not tested by the sources) is that GPT-4 in research-article mode emits formulaic "we" in announcement moves ("In this study, we propose") but not in evaluative or decision-taking moves ("we chose X because", "we read this as"). The useful test is therefore functional, not a count.
- Detection test (stance map): for each paragraph, mark the main claim's commitment marker (hedge, booster, attitude, or none) and whether the author is the visible agent of any judgment. A section where most claim sentences carry no stance marker and the only "we" instances are announcements reads as the LLM profile.
- Rewrite rule: put the author's judgment at the decision points ("We excluded X because ...", "We read this difference as ...", "This is weaker evidence than ..."), and hedge only where the evidence is actually uncertain. Do not sprinkle hedges or "clearly/indeed" to sound human; prompted stance overshoots on boosters (Crosthwaite 2026).
- Detection test (bundle rigidity): repeated noun/preposition bundles used as connective scaffolding ("in the context of", "the role of", "in terms of the", "a wide range of", "in order to") with few epistemic bundles ("it is possible that", "this suggests that", "we argue that") indicate the formulaic profile.
- COVERAGE: Commandment 12 ("size each claim", "one hedge per claim") and R6 ("one hedge max") push toward fewer hedges, which matches the LLM direction rather than correcting it. NOT COVERED: (a) stance deficit and absent authorial presence as a tell; (b) booster overshoot when stance is added; (c) bundle rigidity. The skills should distinguish "calibrated hedge on uncertain evidence" (wanted) from "stacked filler hedges" (banned).

### Gaps
- No study found that isolates Claude-family models on academic metadiscourse; most corpora are ChatGPT/GPT-4. The 2026 CARS study (section 2) is the exception for structure.
- No quantified study of metadiscourse in LLM-written methods sections specifically.

## 2. Section-specific tells: abstracts, introductions, literature reviews, discussion/limitations, conclusions

### Takeaway
The strongest section-level evidence is structural. LLM introductions open on a centrality claim far more often than humans, almost never open on the literature or on the present study, and fill every rhetorical move and step of the standard template; a blind reader caught 90% of generated introductions using structure alone. LLM literature reviews report papers instead of synthesizing them. LLM use is densest in Discussion paragraphs and abstracts, lowest in Methods.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG, introductions. 2026 study of research-article introductions under Swales' CARS model (linguistics papers; generators GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemma-4-31B, Qwen3.5-27B): 46% of human introductions open with a centrality claim and 32% with the literature; models open with centrality in 58-84% and with the literature in 0-12%. Zero of 608 generated introductions open on Move 3 (the present study). Humans use 2.67 of 3 moves and 5.3 of 10 steps on average, and 28% omit a move; most generated sets realize all three moves and 6.1-7.4 steps. Human step self-similarity 0.33 with 36 distinct move structures; generated 0.37-0.46 with 8-35 structures. "Indicating a gap" is 9.6% of human segments vs 12.4-13.7% for Claude and 13.0-16.0% for Qwen; "question raising" 2.8% human vs up to 6.3%. A blind reader identified 108/120 (90%) correctly and relied on argumentation structure in 118/120 judgments and style in 15/120. Prompting the models with the CARS model made them easier to spot (30/30 identified). [arXiv 2610.01353](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01353v1)
- STRONG, abstracts. Gao et al. (2023, npj Digital Medicine): blinded reviewers identified 68% of ChatGPT abstracts and misflagged 14% of real ones; suspected generated abstracts were "vaguer and more formulaic", "superficial and vague"; generated abstracts carried "completely generated data". [npj Digital Medicine](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41746-023-00819-6)
- STRONG, abstracts vocabulary. Kobak et al. (2025, Science Advances): 15 million PubMed abstracts; at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed, up to 40% in some subcorpora; of 379 excess style words, 66% were verbs and 14% adjectives; common markers with largest excess include "potential" (+5.2 points), "findings" (+4.1), "crucial" (+3.7); the 10-word estimator set is "across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within"; rare markers "delves" (r=28.0), "underscores" (13.8), "showcasing" (10.7). [Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/full/10.1126/sciadv.adt3813)
- STRONG, by section. Follow-up on PubMed Central full texts: by December 2025, 89% of papers show excess LLM vocabulary; a Discussion paragraph is about twice as likely to be LLM-assisted (68%) as a Methods paragraph (32%), though Methods prevalence overall exceeds 50%; the word "these" appeared in 50% of December 2025 abstracts vs 33% expected. [arXiv 2608.10715](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10715v1)
- STRONG, literature reviews. Benchmark against a 102-article manual review: "The tools reported the literature; they did not synthesize it"; syntheses "largely accurate" but expert reviewers "found little insight". [arXiv 2611.05459](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.05459)
- MODERATE, literature reviews. Evaluation of LLM-generated reviews across context-window sizes: "descriptive summaries of the previous works rather than analytical synthesis"; longer reviews gained length "by mentioning more prior articles without offering substantive critical evaluations"; larger context windows "exacerbate issues such as content repetition, omission of critical work, and a tendency towards descriptiveness over synthesis". [arXiv 2608.26145](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26145)
- MODERATE, literature reviews (qualitative, LSE). Lahlou et al. (2026): AI reviews had "no direct quotations, statistical data was rarely mentioned, and page references were missing altogether"; "struggled to distinguish between stronger and weaker studies"; papers "sorted into categories, but there is no logical link between them nor guiding analytic structure"; "connectors juxtaposing similar findings or contrasting different findings, but without deep causal analysis". [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/137815/1/2026_AI_and_literature_reviews._Lahlou_et_al._2603.20235v1.pdf)
- MODERATE, literature reviews (Claude). Library-science comparison of a human review and a Claude review: good at "summarizing points of agreement across sources", weak at synthesis; when combining elements from multiple articles it "failed to correctly attribute the source of the information"; "unable to think through the utility of the citations in bolstering an argument". [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/article/961199)
- MODERATE, systematic reviews. GPT-4-based Consensus vs a manual SLR: precision 76.2%, recall 38.1%; none of the 32 manually selected primary studies appeared in the AI results; "limited critical analysis". [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10938577/)

### Inferences
- Detection test (introduction opening): if sentence 1 asserts the topic's importance or growth ("In recent years, X has attracted increasing attention", "X is central to ..."), the introduction matches the 58-84% LLM profile. Rewrite rule: open on the specific problem, the specific prior finding the paper builds on, or (where the venue allows) the present study's question.
- Detection test (template completeness): list the CARS steps present. An introduction that has every step (generalization, review, gap, question, purpose, findings, value, structure) is over-complete. Rewrite rule: keep only the steps this reader needs; a well-known problem needs no centrality paragraph. This is commandment 8 applied to moves.
- Detection test (gap inflation): count gap statements ("However, little is known", "remains underexplored", "To our knowledge, no study has"). More than one per introduction, or a gap that the cited literature does not demonstrate, matches the over-used gap step. Rewrite rule: one gap, stated with the specific prior work that leaves it open.
- Detection test (literature review synthesis): for each paragraph, count sentences whose subject is a single cited work ("Smith et al. found ..."). Three or more in sequence with only "Similarly", "In addition", "In contrast" between them is report-by-paper. Second test: does the paragraph say which study is stronger and why (design, sample size, measure)? Third: does any sentence carry a specific number, quote, or page from a source? Absence of all three is the LLM review profile (Lahlou 2026).
- Rewrite rule (synthesis): organize the review by claim or by disagreement, not by paper; each paragraph's first sentence states what the literature collectively shows or where it splits; cite studies as evidence for that sentence with one discriminating detail each (design, n, effect).
- The Discussion is where LLM text concentrates (68% of paragraphs in PMC 2025). That is the section to audit hardest for unasked rebuttals, generic implications, and recap.
- COVERAGE: commandment 3 (connection), 2 (paragraph point), 8 (selection) and 11 (concrete case) address the symptoms in general. NOT COVERED: (a) centrality-claim opening as an introduction tell; (b) move/step over-completeness; (c) gap-statement inflation; (d) report-by-paper literature review and the "no specifics, no strength ranking" tests; (e) section-targeted audit priority (Discussion first). no-ai-tells covers "has become increasingly important" but not "has garnered/attracted (significant|increasing) attention", "In recent years," as sentence-1 opener, or "remains (largely) underexplored".

### Gaps
- I found no quantified study of "Future research should explore ..." or limitation boilerplate frequency in LLM vs human papers. Several search hits were themselves papers using the phrase, which is anecdotal evidence of its ubiquity in human writing too; it is not a reliable LLM-specific tell.
- No quantified study found on LLM conclusion recaps or contribution-list bullets in papers (as opposed to general recap closes).
- Kobak markers like "these", "potential", "across", "within" are corpus-level signals; they cannot be banned in individual texts without false positives.

## 3. Peer review tells

### Takeaway
LLM reviews are longer, more positive (higher scores), more alike across papers, and more generic: they comment on "implications" and request "more datasets" rather than judging novelty or asking for targeted ablations, and they rarely tie criticism to a section, figure, or equation. They are more frequent near deadlines and from low-confidence reviewers, and carry praise adjectives (commendable, meticulous, intricate) and formatting habits (bold 2-3 word header tags).

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Liang et al. (2024, ICML), AI-conference reviews: 6.5-16.9% of review text substantially LLM-modified (10.6% of ICLR 2024 sentences, 16.9% EMNLP 2023), no significant evidence in Nature portfolio reviews; "commendable", "meticulous", "intricate" show 9.8-, 34.7- and 11.2-fold increases per sentence; estimated LLM share higher in reviews reporting lower confidence, within 3 days of the deadline (ICLR 8.8% vs 11.3%), and from reviewers less likely to respond to rebuttals. Appendix top-100 AI-favored adjectives include, beyond no-ai-tells' list: laudable, lucid, cogent, methodical, ingenious, potent, appreciable, admirable, refreshing, intriguing, thoughtful, credible, exceptional, insightful, compelling, substantive, demonstrable, unprecedented. [arXiv 2403.07183](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07183v1); adjective table at [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2403.07183)
- STRONG. Liang et al. (2024, NEJM AI), GPT-4 feedback on 3,096 Nature-family and 1,709 ICLR papers: GPT-4 comments on implications 7.27 times more often than humans and is 10.69 times less likely to comment on novelty; humans are 6.71 times more likely to request ablations, GPT-4 2.19 times more likely to request experiments on more datasets; GPT-4 "often struggles to provide in-depth critique of method design"; the most important limitation is "its ability to generate specific and actionable feedback". [arXiv 2310.01783](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.01783); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10617069)
- MODERATE (vendor analysis, contested). Pangram Labs on ICLR 2026: 21% (15,899) of 75,800 reviews fully AI-generated, over half with some AI involvement; more AI in a review correlates with higher scores; AI reviews are longer with "filler content"; tells include "bold section headers with 2-3 word summary tags followed by a colon", "shallow nit-picks" such as requests for ablations "very similar to the ablations presented", larger test sets, "more clarification or more examples". [Pangram analysis PDF](https://finkelsteinlab.org/assets/pdfs/blog/brandolinis-world/pangram-iclr-2026-reviews.pdf). A 2026 evaluation argues the 21% figure "may not be accurate" because Pangram classifies most mixed-authorship reviews as "AI" and has a 3.1% false-positive rate on some human review sets. [arXiv 2603.20450](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.20450)
- STRONG. "Hivemind" analysis of the same 75,800 ICLR 2026 reviews: fully AI-generated reviews are more similar to each other across different papers (mean cosine 0.486 vs 0.467, d=0.29), rising to d=0.35 in the weaknesses and questions sections; significant in all 21 primary areas; AI reviews average 507 words vs 424; 50 of 58 reviews that authors publicly accused of being AI were flagged as fully AI by Pangram. [arXiv 2605.03202](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03202v2)
- MODERATE. "How LLMs Distort Our Written Language" (2026): LLM-generated reviews "place significantly less weight on clarity and significance of the research, and assign scores that, on average, are a full point higher". [arXiv 2603.18161](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161v1)
- WEAK (anecdote, reported in Nature). A referee report received by ecologist Timothee Poisot contained "Here is a revised version of your review with improved clarity and structure". [Nature news feature, March 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-00894-7)
- MODERATE (editorial, Canadian Journal of Chemical Engineering 2026). Defective reviews "make broad claims without evidence", "criticize the manuscript without citing specific sections, figures, equations, assumptions, or interpretations", "contain confident statements that do not correspond to what the authors actually wrote", and "ask for changes that are vague, impossible, irrelevant, or disproportionate"; AI-generated reviews "may identify weaknesses that are not present, miss the central contribution". The "Research Director Fallacy" ("The authors should have studied a different system...") is named as a separate defect. [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cjce.70497)
- MODERATE. Detection is unreliable for individual reviews: Nature reports that tools "fail to identify most AI-generated peer-review reports". [Nature Index, Dec 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-04032-1)

### Inferences
- Detection test (anchoring): every weakness should name a location (section, table, figure, equation, line) and a consequence for the paper's claim. A weakness with neither is the generic profile.
- Detection test (novelty judgment): does the review state whether the contribution is new relative to named prior work? LLM reviews comment on novelty 10.69 times less often. Rewrite rule: one sentence on novelty with the closest prior work named.
- Detection test (request type): "evaluate on more datasets", "provide more details", "discuss broader implications", "add more examples" without saying which dataset, which detail, or what the result would change are the LLM request profile. Rewrite rule: request the specific ablation or control that would falsify the claim, and say what outcome would change the recommendation.
- Detection test (praise and template): opening paragraph that restates the abstract, then "Strengths:" with praise adjectives, then bolded 2-3 word tag headers ("**Limited Evaluation:**"), then score above the reviewer's text tone. Rewrite rule: summary in two sentences in the reviewer's own framing, then the one or two issues that decide the recommendation, in order of weight.
- Length is not quality: AI reviews are about 20% longer on average.
- COVERAGE: no-ai-tells bans commendable, meticulous, intricate, notable, noteworthy, invaluable, pivotal, versatile, comprehensive, innovative, holistic, seamless, and bans header/bullet inflation (R5.6). serious-academic lists "reviews" in scope but has no review-specific rules. NOT COVERED: (a) location anchoring; (b) novelty judgment; (c) generic request types; (d) abstract-restating summary; (e) bold tag-colon headers specifically; (f) score/tone inflation; (g) the second-tier praise adjectives (laudable, lucid, cogent, methodical, ingenious, admirable, refreshing, intriguing, insightful, compelling, commendably); (h) leakage "Here is a revised version of your review".

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative study of the phrase "the authors should consider" in LLM vs human reviews; it is a plausible tell but unsourced.
- No quantified evidence found for biomedical or humanities journal reviews comparable to the ML conference data (Nature portfolio showed no significant signal in 2024).

## 4. Citation behaviors: fabrication, misattribution, vague support, citation bias

### Takeaway
Fabricated references are now measurable in accepted top-venue papers (about 1 in 20 NeurIPS 2025 papers has at least two likely hallucinated references; ACL-family papers with hallucinated references rose from 0.28% in 2024 to 2.59% in 2025, per [Sakai et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.18724) Table 4). Beyond fabrication, LLM references are often real but wrong in metadata, attached to the wrong claim during synthesis, and skewed toward highly cited, short-titled canonical works.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Walters and Wilder (2023, Scientific Reports): 636 references in 84 generated literature reviews; 55% of GPT-3.5 and 18% of GPT-4 references fabricated; across six prior studies 51% of 732 citations fabricated. [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)
- MODERATE. Bhattacharyya et al. (2023, Cureus): of 115 ChatGPT-3.5 references in medical papers, 47% fabricated, 46% authentic but inaccurate, 7% authentic and accurate. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10277170/)
- MODERATE. Chelli et al. (2024, JMIR): hallucination rates 39.6% (GPT-3.5), 28.6% (GPT-4), 91.4% (Bard) for systematic-review references; precision against the real reviews 9.4%, 13.4%, 0%. [JMIR](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e53164/)
- MODERATE, current models. Neurocritical care (2026): GPT-5.3, DeepSeek-V3, Grok-4, retrieval disabled, 300 references: 55.0% contained an inaccuracy and 28.3% were completely fabricated; GPT-5.3 fabricated 27%. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42640622/)
- STRONG, in the published record. "Phantom References" (2026): reference-level rates below 1% (2025: ICLR 0.38%, ICML 0.54%, NeurIPS 0.68%, USENIX Security 0.81%), but roughly one in twenty accepted NeurIPS and USENIX Security 2025 papers has at least two likely hallucinated academic references; ICLR 2026 is the highest ICLR rate observed (3.1% on all references). Cites Naser (2026): 69,557 citations across ten models, 11.4-56.8% hallucination depending on model and domain. [arXiv 2607.00738](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00738)
- STRONG. Reference-hallucination study summarizing GhostCite (Xu et al. 2026: 13 LLMs, 40 domains, 375K citations, 14-95% hallucination, a 51-point spread across domains) and Sakai et al. (2026: 295 of 17,842 ACL/NAACL/EMNLP papers with hallucinated references, 0.28% of papers in 2024 to 2.59% in 2025); 3-13% of citation URLs hallucinated. [arXiv 2604.03173](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03173)
- STRONG. Algaba et al. (2025, NAACL Findings/ACL Anthology): LLM reference suggestions show "a more pronounced high citation bias", persisting after controlling for year, title length, author count and venue; holds for GPT-4, GPT-4o and Claude 3.5. [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/people/andres-algaba/); [arXiv 2405.15739](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15739v1)
- STRONG. Follow-up on 274,951 GPT-4o references for 10,000 papers: LLMs "systematically reinforce the Matthew effect", preferring highly cited, more recent, shorter-titled, fewer-author works. [arXiv 2504.02767](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.02767)
- MODERATE. Misattribution in synthesis: Claude, combining points from several articles, "failed to correctly attribute the source of the information". [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/article/961199)
- MODERATE (case). PLOS One retracted Shoukat et al. (2024) 45 days after publication: "Regenerate response" in reference 62 led to the finding that 18 of 76 references could not be identified. [Glynn, arXiv 2503.19848](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.19848)

### Inferences
- The failure that survives a fabrication check is the "authentic but inaccurate" reference (46% in Bhattacharyya) and the real paper attached to a claim it does not make. A DOI resolver catches the first category, not the second.
- Detection test (claim-source fit): for each in-text citation, open the source and find the page or table supporting the specific sentence. A clump citation ("[3-9]" or "(A; B; C; D)") supporting a general sentence ("Several studies have explored X") fails this test by construction, because no single source supports the sentence as written.
- Detection test (canon skew): if nearly every reference is a highly cited review or a landmark paper and none is a recent, specific empirical study, the list matches the LLM citation-bias profile. Rewrite rule: cite the specific empirical source for each specific claim; cite a review only for "the field broadly holds X".
- Rewrite rule (clumps): replace "Several studies have shown X [3-9]" with the two or three studies that matter, each with what it found and how ("A (n=..., design) found X; B found the opposite under condition Y").
- COVERAGE: serious-academic already says never invent a citation and check each cited claim against the source; no-ai-tells R5.4 bans vague attribution ("experts say"). NOT COVERED: (a) "authentic but inaccurate" metadata (check authors, year, venue, title, not just existence); (b) citation clumps as a structural tell; (c) canon/Matthew-effect skew; (d) misattribution during cross-source synthesis; (e) "studies have shown" with no named study (the academic form of vague attribution, worth adding to the phrase list: "studies have shown", "research has shown", "a growing body of literature", "extensive research has").

### Gaps
- No quantified study found on citation placement (sentence-final clumps vs integral citations) in LLM vs human papers; the clump tell rests on the synthesis-failure evidence, not direct measurement.
- No study found measuring over-citation of review articles specifically by LLMs, beyond the general high-citation bias.

## 5. Argumentative and epistemic failures

### Takeaway
LLM argument converges on the same few main arguments, supports them with "hedged generality", extends rebuttal across consecutive paragraphs, and moves fast to proposals. When summarizing science, LLMs widen the scope of findings (past-tense sample-specific results become generic present-tense claims) about five times as often as human summaries, and accuracy prompts make it worse. LLM editing pushes stated positions toward neutral. Together these produce the profile the professor described: evenhanded, under-committed, rebutting objections nobody raised, while overstating scope.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Kim, Chang, Pham and Iyyer (2026), "Argument Collapse": 1,039 NYT debate responses, 448 Boston Review responses, 23,384 LLM essays. 65.3% of human main arguments are unique within a debate vs 3.4% of LLM main arguments; within the same main argument 41.0% of human sub-arguments are unique vs 9.1%; "LLMs often reuse generalized and hedged sub-arguments, while humans prefer more concrete and topic-specific ones". Paragraph transitions: rebuttal followed by rebuttal 52.0% for default LLMs vs 32.4% humans; support followed by support 29.7% vs 54.5%; support followed by proposal 17.7% vs 7.2%. Authors caution the corpus is public debate, not research writing. [arXiv 2606.01736](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.01736v1)
- MODERATE (secondary summary of the same paper). When asked to vary widely, 66.4% of LLM essays took a strong stance vs 76.1% of human essays. [ETC Journal](https://etcjournal.com/2026/10/29/ai-seems-unable-to-take-a-stand-on-controversial-issues-true/)
- STRONG. "How LLMs Distort Our Written Language" (2026): heavy LLM use produced a 68.9% increase in essays that "remained neutral in answering the topic question" (p=0.017); LLM edits shift essays "in the same direction regardless of model" and "argue for entirely different conclusions"; LLM edits roughly double positive and negative sentiment. [arXiv 2603.18161](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18161v1)
- STRONG. Peters and Chin-Yee (2025, Royal Society Open Science): 4,900 summaries from 10 LLMs; most models overgeneralized; DeepSeek, ChatGPT-4o and LLaMA 3.3 70B in 26-73% of cases; LLM summaries nearly five times more likely than human summaries to contain broad generalizations (OR 4.85, 95% CI 3.06-7.70); newer models did worse. [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12042776/). Example of the shift: "The treatment was effective in this study" becomes "The treatment is effective"; prompts asking for accuracy made overgeneralization nearly twice as likely. [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2025-05-prominent-chatbots-routinely-exaggerate-science.html)
- STRONG, novelty/hype baseline. Millar, Batalo and Budgell (2023, JAMA Network Open): in 2.39 million PubMed abstracts of NIH-funded research 1985-2020, 133 of 139 hype words increased; "novel" +803%, "key" +1327%, "transformative" +4489%, "unprecedented" +2567%, "crucial" +1123%, "groundbreaking" +621%. This predates LLMs. [JAMA Netw Open commentary](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2813190)
- MODERATE. Hype-annotation study on NIH abstracts: "groundbreaking, revolutionary, unparalleled, unprecedented" were hype in 100% of uses; "innovative" 82%, "creative" and "unique" 68%; "novel" and "first" vary by context. [arXiv 2509.24638](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2509.24638)
- MODERATE. Debate-to-Write (COLING 2025): LLM arguments "rely heavily on reasoning without sufficient evidential support", e.g., a claim headed "Financial Sustainability and Conservation" with no concrete example or research. [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.314.pdf)

### Inferences
- The professor's two complaints map onto measured behavior. "Lists of facts without connecting them" matches report-by-paper reviews (section 2) and support-to-support transitions at about half the human rate. "Rebut a rejection that wasn't revealed" matches rebuttal-to-rebuttal transitions at 52.0% vs 32.4%. The rebuttal finding is from public-debate essays, so its transfer to papers is an inference.
- Detection test (scope drift): for every result claim, compare tense, quantifier and population against the result as measured. Past-tense, sample-specific results restated as present-tense generics ("X improves Y", "users prefer") are scope inflation. Rewrite rule: keep the sample, condition and tense ("In our sample of 145 apps, X occurred in 31%").
- Detection test (portability): could this argument, limitation or implication appear unchanged in another paper on the same topic? If yes, it is a collapsed or generic argument. Rewrite rule: tie it to a number, design choice or case from this paper.
- Detection test (proposal rush): a paragraph that moves from one support sentence to "Practitioners should ..." or "Policymakers should ..." matches the fast support-to-proposal arc. Rewrite rule: only state an implication the results license, with the result that licenses it.
- Detection test (neutral drift after editing): after any LLM polish pass, diff each claim sentence's commitment against the author's draft. A claim that went from "X causes Y" to "X may be associated with Y" or from a stated position to "both perspectives have merit" was changed in meaning, not style.
- Hype is a human trend that LLMs extend; the vocabulary rule should target unsupported novelty claims ("novel", "first", "unique") in addition to the 100%-hype words, with the test "what prior work is it compared to?"
- COVERAGE: commandment 5 (no unasked rebuttals), 12 (size claims; qualify "first", "largest", "all"), no-ai-tells R5.3 (significance inflation) and bans on groundbreaking, innovative, paradigm, transformative, unprecedented-like words (unprecedented is not on the R2 list). NOT COVERED: (a) scope drift via tense and generic present (the Peters pattern); (b) portability test for collapsed arguments; (c) proposal rush and generic implications for practitioners/policymakers; (d) neutral drift after LLM editing; (e) "unprecedented", "unparalleled", "revolutionary", "first-ever", "novel" without a named comparison; (f) evenhandedness without commitment ("both approaches have strengths and limitations").

### Gaps
- No study found that measures falsifiability or presence of a testable claim in LLM vs human papers.
- No quantified study found of false precision (spurious decimals, invented effect sizes) in LLM academic prose beyond Gao's "completely generated data" in abstracts.
- Argument-collapse evidence is from public debate essays; no equivalent study of research-paper discussion sections was found.

## 6. What publishers, editors and integrity trackers report as giveaways

### Takeaway
The documented giveaways in published papers are mostly chatbot leakage: knowledge-cutoff disclaimers (about half of Academ-AI cases), "Certainly, here is", "Regenerate response", first-person singular and second-person address in multi-author papers, "I don't have access to real-time data", and referrals to "recent literature" or professionals. Masked variants ("as of my last knowledge" with the date removed) also appear. Funders now treat substantial AI drafting as non-original (NIH, from September 2025).

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Glynn, Academ-AI (first 768 examples): undeclared AI appears in high-citation, high-APC journals; 33 documents (4.3%) altered post publication; "Regenerate response" in 61 cases (8%); "can/cannot provide" 76 (9.9%); "access" 54 (7.0%). Search patterns: "as an AI language model", "certainly, here are", "don't have access to real-time". [arXiv 2411.15218](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.15218)
- STRONG. Earlier Academ-AI analysis of 500 examples: 49.0% referred to a knowledge cutoff; feature patterns include first-person singular ("I recommend", "I apologize", "I must"), second-person address ("you", "your"), "lack of access" ("real-time information"), self-identification as a language model, and referral to other sources ("recent", "latest", "up-to-date"); about one in five evaded detection at a publisher with an explicit policy. [ar5iv 2411.15218](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2411.15218)
- STRONG. Strzelecki (2025, Learned Publishing): "as of my last knowledge update" and "regenerate response" found in premier journals; some authors masked the phrase by deleting the month and year and leaving "as of my last knowledge". [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/leap.1650)
- STRONG. Haider et al. (2024, HKS Misinformation Review): 139 Google Scholar papers with suspected deceptive GPT use found via "as of my last knowledge update" and "I don't have access to real-time data"; about 62% did not declare GPT use; 57% on policy-relevant subjects. [HKS Misinformation Review](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/gpt-fabricated-scientific-papers-on-google-scholar-key-features-spread-and-implications-for-preempting-evidence-manipulation/)
- MODERATE. Chronicle of Higher Education quoting an Academ-AI case: "It's important to note that advancements in HIV research occur regularly, and new findings may have emerged since my last update in January 2022. For the most current information, it's recommended to consult recent scientific literature or speak with healthcare professionals"; about 2% of cases formally corrected, about 1% "stealth" corrected. [Chronicle](https://www.chronicle.com/article/scholars-are-supposed-to-say-when-they-use-ai-do-they)
- STRONG (policy). NIH NOT-OD-25-132: applications "substantially developed by AI, or contain[ing] sections substantially developed by AI" are not considered original; cap of six applications per PI per calendar year, effective September 25, 2025; NIH cites PIs submitting more than 40 distinct applications in one round. [NIH Grants](https://grants.nih.gov/grants/guide/notice-files/NOT-OD-25-132.html)

### Inferences
- Detection test (leakage grep, academic additions): first-person singular in a multi-author paper; "you/your" outside quoted material; "consult (recent|the latest) (literature|research)|a (healthcare )?professional"; "may have emerged since"; "real-time (data|information|access)"; "as of my last"; "Certainly, here"; "Regenerate response"; "Here is a revised version"; "improved clarity and structure"; "[Insert", "[Author Name]", "[Citation needed]" placeholders.
- COVERAGE: no-ai-tells R3 LEAKAGE covers "as an AI/large language model", knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and reader sign-offs. NOT COVERED: "Certainly, here", "Regenerate response", masked cutoff phrases, first-person singular in multi-author work, second-person address in a paper, "consult a professional/recent literature" referrals, "real-time" access disclaimers, the review-polish leakage line.

### Gaps
- COPE and individual publisher policy pages were not fetched in this pass; I cannot quote their lists of giveaways. Publisher policies mostly concern disclosure rather than listing textual tells.
- No source found that lists unfilled template brackets as a documented giveaway in papers; that pattern is plausible but unsourced here.

## 7. Field differences (CS/ML vs biomedical vs humanities/social science)

### Takeaway
LLM use is highest in computer science (22.5% of abstract sentences by September 2024) and lowest in mathematics and Nature portfolio papers (under 10%); review papers carry more LLM text than empirical papers; within biomedical papers the Discussion carries twice the LLM density of Methods. Fabricated-reference rates vary by tens of points across domains. Humanities and social-science evidence is thinner and mostly comes from applied linguistics.

### Cited Findings
- STRONG. Liang et al. (2025, Nature Human Behaviour): 1,121,912 papers; by September 2024 LLM-modified sentences reached 22.5% of CS abstracts and 19.6% of CS introductions, vs 7.7% and 4.1% in mathematics and 8.9% and 9.4% in the Nature portfolio; higher among first authors who post more preprints (22.9% vs 20.0%), in crowded areas, and in shorter papers (22.0% vs 19.3%). [Stanford PDF](https://nlp.stanford.edu/~manning/papers/Liang_et_al-2025-Nature_Human_Behaviour.pdf)
- STRONG. arXiv review vs non-review papers (2026): CS review papers 21.4% vs non-review 14.0% LLM-generated (alpha estimator, 2023-2025); by Pangram, CS review papers rose from 7.4% (2023) to 43.3% (2025) vs 2.7% to 23.3% for non-review papers. [arXiv 2601.17036](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.17036)
- STRONG. Biomedical full texts: Discussion paragraphs 68% vs Methods 32% LLM-assisted (December 2025 PMC). [arXiv 2608.10715](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10715v1)
- MODERATE. Cybersecurity top-4 venues: pronounced post-2022 increase in marker-word usage across all venues, plus a long-run drift to higher lexical complexity. [arXiv 2604.09316](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.09316)
- STRONG. Fabrication varies by domain: GhostCite 14-95% across 40 domains (51-point domain spread). [arXiv 2604.03173](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03173). Early small studies: geography 94% (n=18) vs medicine 17-69%. [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5)
- MODERATE, humanities/applied linguistics. In discussion and conclusion sections of applied linguistics articles, hedges were the most frequent interactional marker in both corpora, consistent with "humanities writing often relies on cautious or tentative claims", and human authors used significantly more of them. [Shiraz TESL](https://tesl.shirazu.ac.ir/article_8833_bd7da341b6c638e9f62814a8620ce174.pdf)
- STRONG, peer review by venue. No significant ChatGPT signal in Nature portfolio reviews in 2024, vs 10.6-16.9% at ML/NLP conferences. [arXiv 2403.07183](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07183v1)

### Inferences
- In CS/ML, the tells reviewers will meet most are in abstracts, introductions (centrality openings, contribution announcements), and reviews themselves; venue norms already include contribution bullets, so a contribution list is not a tell there, but a generic one is.
- In biomedicine, the audit priority is the Discussion (scope drift and generic implications) and the reference list (fabricated or inaccurate references in clinical topics).
- In humanities and social science, where stance and authorial voice carry the argument, the stance deficit and neutral drift (sections 1 and 5) matter most; hedging norms are higher, so the "one hedge per claim" default in no-ai-tells R6 is too strict there if read as a quota.
- COVERAGE: neither skill has field-specific guidance. NOT COVERED: section audit priority by field; hedge norms by discipline.

### Gaps
- No quantified study found on LLM tells in humanities monographs, philosophy papers, or history; the humanities evidence is from applied linguistics and student essays.
- No study found comparing grant-proposal prose (NIH/NSF/ERC) for LLM tells beyond NIH's policy statement; the hype-in-NIH-applications literature predates LLMs.
