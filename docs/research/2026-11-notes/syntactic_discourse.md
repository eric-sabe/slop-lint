# Syntactic, grammatical, rhetorical, and discourse-level LLM tells (beyond slop-lint 0.9.1)

Baseline read before research (so the report writer knows what "not covered" means):
- `slop-lint.mjs` 0.9.1 is strictly line-by-line: `lintText` splits on `\n` and runs WORDS and PHRASES regexes per line. There is no sentence splitter, no per-document counter, and no rate or density metric anywhere. Every measured-rate finding below therefore needs a new document-level pass (count per 1k words or per 100 sentences, compare to a threshold), not another PHRASES entry.
- PHRASES covers: negated contrast family (not just X but Y; it's not X, it's Y; X isn't the A, it's B; A isn't the X. It is the Y.; doesn't X, it just Y), modal antithesis, aphorism molds, corrective anaphora, "the real question", "Which means" fragment opener, validate-then-restate and compliment openers, "here's the thing/why", conclusion rituals, "I hope this helps".
- SKILL.md R1.5/R1.6 (bold, semicolon density) and R5 (rule of three, participial tail, synonym cycling, header/bullet inflation, recap close, praise-before-answer, offer-of-help close) are "check by reading" with no numeric threshold.

## Q1. Biber-style multidimensional findings (Reinhart et al. PNAS 2025 and others): which grammatical features are over/under-used, by how much, per model, and is instruction tuning the driver?

### Takeaway
Instruction-tuned models write in a denser, more nominal, more "informational/explicit-reference" register than humans in the same genre: GPT-4o uses present participial clauses at 5.3x the human rate and nominalizations at 2.1x, while using agentless passives at about half the human rate; base models are much closer to human, so instruction tuning (and long system prompts) is the main driver. Two of these (participial clauses, nominalizations) are cheap to approximate with regex and are the strongest calibrated additions available.

### Cited Findings
- Reinhart, Markey, Laudenbach, Pantusen, Yurko, Weinberg, Brown, "Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in grammatical and rhetorical styles", PNAS 122 (2025) e2422455122. Parallel corpora built from prompts drawn from human text: HAP-E (8,290 texts / 66,320 chunks; academic, news, fiction, spoken, blogs, TV/movie scripts) and CAP from COCA (9,615 texts / 76,920 chunks). Models: GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, Llama 3 8B and 70B, base and instruct. Features: Biber's 66-ish lexical/grammatical/rhetorical tags. — [arXiv 2410.16107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16107); [PNAS DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422455122)
- Present participial clauses: GPT-4o at 5.3x the human rate (Cohen's d = 1.38); instruction-tuned models overall at 2 to 5x. — [Reinhart et al., HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Nominalizations: GPT-4o 2.1x (d = 1.23); instruction-tuned models overall 1.5 to 2x. — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- "That" clauses in subject position: GPT-4o 2.6x (d = 0.77). (The summary I retrieved labels it "that clauses as subject"; in Biber's tag set the nearest feature is that-relatives on subject position, so confirm the exact feature in SI Table S5 before quoting.) — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Phrasal coordination ("X and Y" joining words/phrases, not clauses): GPT-4o 1.9x (d = 0.81). — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Agentless passives: GPT-4o about 0.5x human (under-use); Llama base models near human rates. — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Family-specific, opposite-direction features: both GPT-4o models avoid clausal coordination while all Llama 3 variants over-use it; both GPT-4o models over-use downtoners (barely, nearly) while all Llama 3 variants avoid them. — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Vocabulary overrepresentation (Table 1, ratio vs human): camaraderie 162x (GPT-4o) / 171x (mini); tapestry 155x / 147x; intricate 119x / 129x; palpable 95x / 145x; unease 101x (Llama 3 8B Instruct) / 63x (70B Instruct). — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Differences persist as models get larger and are larger for instruction-tuned than base models. — [Reinhart et al. abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16107)
- Classification on Biber features alone: 7-way random forest 66% (chance 14%); pairwise human-vs-one-LLM 93 to 98%, including cross-corpus; Llama base models about 75% with lasso; transfer to M4 arXiv abstracts (GPT-3.5) fell to chance (50%) for Llama-base-trained models. Only 4.2% of LLM texts classed as human; 9.8% of human texts classed as LLM. — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- Per-feature rate tables (per 1k words) are in SI Tables S5 to S6, not in the main text; contractions, first/second person, that-deletion, split auxiliaries, emphatics, and hedges are not reported numerically in the main text. — [Reinhart et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16107v2)
- "Benchmark of stylistic variation in LLM-generated texts" (2025, English BE-21/Brown family and Czech Koditex; models from davinci-002 to GPT-4o, Claude 3/3.5/4, Gemini 1.5/2.0, DeepSeek v3, Llama 3.1 405B): most models shift toward the informational pole of Biber Dimension 1 and toward explicit reference on Dimension 3; Dimension 3 is the most consistent shift across all models. Base/completion models (davinci-002, Llama 3.1 completion) matched human style best. GPT-3.5-turbo with the long ChatGPT system prompt clustered with GPT-4/4-turbo/4o, not with GPT-3.5-turbo under a short prompt. Temperature (0 vs 1) had no systematic effect. — [arXiv 2509.10179](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10179v1)
- Berber Sardinha (2024, Applied Corpus Linguistics), Biber 1988 five-dimension comparison: ignoring register, AI and human differ significantly on 3 of 5 dimensions; controlling register, all F-tests are highly significant, and discriminant analysis identifies AI text from dimension scores "with relative ease". A follow-up on EFL textbook texts finds AI texts more informational, abstract, and impersonal. — [Bohrium abstract record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/ai-generated-vs-human-authored-texts-a-multidimensional-comparison/954390005856337996-86611); [follow-up full text](https://artefacts-discovery.researcher.life/full_text/DA-2/98/98824dd868f93922863562f5cd3fa2ab/full_text/9c0a428163882ea40e012460a8be5012.pdf)
- Herbold et al., Scientific Reports 13:18617 (2023), argumentative essays: ChatGPT essays use more nominalizations, have higher sentence complexity and greater lexical diversity, and fewer discourse and epistemic markers than student essays; ChatGPT-3 had lower lexical diversity than humans, ChatGPT-4 higher. — [arXiv 2304.14276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276v1); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/e7968bb9653c429abb439c08c376647c)

### Inferences
- Instruction tuning is the driver, and system prompts amplify it: three independent groups (Reinhart; the 2509.10179 benchmark; Idiosyncrasies paper on markdown, Q3) show base models near human and chat/instruct models far from it. Temperature does not fix it, so "lower temperature" advice is not a mitigation.
- The universal direction is "denser, more nominal, more explicit reference" (Biber D1 informational, D3 explicit reference). Features that flip sign by family (clausal coordination, downtoners) are bad universal linter rules; a GPT-tuned rule on downtoners would misfire on Llama-style text and vice versa.
- Agentless-passive under-use is real but cannot drive a linter: absence of a feature is weak evidence and passives are what style guides already tell humans to cut.
- Heuristic sketches (all are doc-level rates; none exist in slop-lint today):
  - **Present participial clauses (extends R5.2 with a number).** Count `,\s+(?!including|according|during|regarding|concerning|considering|following|notwithstanding|nothing|something|anything|everything|being\b)[a-z]{3,}ing\s+(?:the|a|an|its|their|his|her|our|your|this|that|these|those|to|in|on|for|with|from|it|them|us|both|each|new|more)\b` (comma + -ing verb + object/prep) plus sentence-initial `(^|[.?!]\s+)[A-Z][a-z]{2,}ing\b[^.?!,]{1,60},` (fronted participle). Normalize per 100 sentences. Because the gap is 2 to 5x with d = 1.38 for GPT-4o, a "warn if this doc's rate is >2x a human baseline measured on the repo's own human specimen corpus" rule is defensible; I found no published per-1k-word human baseline in the retrieved text (it is in SI S5). FP risk: medium. Fiction and sports/news narration use participles heavily; "-ing" nouns after commas ("..., morning came") slip through; tighten with the object/preposition lookahead.
  - **Nominalization density.** Count `\b[a-z]{4,}(?:tion|sion|ment|ness|ity|ance|ence|ism)s?\b` per 1k words, exclude a stoplist of high-frequency lexicalized forms (nation, station, moment, government, city, business, experience, question, mention, attention). GPT-4o 2.1x, instruct 1.5 to 2x. FP risk: high in academic, legal, and policy prose (Biber D3 is high there for humans too), so the threshold must be register-relative or the rule should only fire in chat/README/blog extensions.
  - **Phrasal coordination.** Count `\b(\w{3,}) and (\w{3,})\b` not followed within 2 tokens by a finite-verb cue (pronoun, "is/are/was/will"), per 1k words. 1.9x for GPT-4o. FP risk: high; binomials ("salt and pepper") are normal. Better as one input to a combined density score than a standalone warning. The paired-adjective subcase ("clear and concise", "simple and effective") is a narrower, lower-FP target.

### Gaps
- Absolute per-1k-word rates for each feature, human and per model, live in PNAS SI Tables S5 to S6, which I did not retrieve. Without them the thresholds above must be calibrated on slop-lint's own specimen corpus.
- No 2025 to 2026 Biber-style replication on GPT-5.x, Claude 4.x/Opus 5.x, Gemini 2.5+/3.x was found. Whether the 5.3x participle rate holds for current frontier models is unknown.
- Contractions, first person, and second person rates for LLM vs human prose were not reported numerically in any source I retrieved. The Biber D1 shift (away from "involved" features such as contractions, private verbs, and 1st/2nd person) predicts under-use in essay/blog prose, but I have no number.
- Berber Sardinha dimension-by-dimension scores and effect sizes were not retrieved.

## Q2. Sentence-length uniformity / burstiness, paragraph-length uniformity, sentence-opening variety, list/heading formatting: measured numbers usable as thresholds?

### Takeaway
LLM prose is measurably "flatter": in one speech corpus the sentence-length coefficient of variation was about 50% for ChatGPT vs about 78% for human presidential addresses, and a 2026 study found 8 of 9 models significantly flatter than matched Wikinews articles, with prompting and few-shot examples not closing the gap. Two calibration warnings matter for any implementation: raw variance is confounded with mean length, and markdown scaffolding (list items, headings) inflates variance enough to mask the signal unless stripped first. Markdown use itself is a strong model fingerprint and is reward-favored.

### Cited Findings
- Sentence-length coefficient of variation (std/mean): French presidential addresses about 78%; ChatGPT-generated speeches about 50%; generated sentence lengths "significantly less diverse". — [ChatGPT as speechwriter for the French presidents, arXiv 2411.18382](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.18382)
- Human vs six open LLMs on news: the LLMs show a similar distribution of sentence lengths with less variation than human sentences (qualitative in the snippet retrieved). — [Muñoz-Ortiz, Gómez-Rodríguez, Vilares, Artificial Intelligence Review, arXiv 2308.09067](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09067)
- Lanzo and Angelo, "Burstiness Was Measured Wrong, and Prompting Cannot Aim It" (NeurIPS 2026 workshop on Linguistic Principles for Foundation Models; abstract-level only): plain sentence-length variance grows with mean sentence length, so they score sentence boundaries as a point process in word-time with the Goh-Barabasi burstiness coefficient plus Kim-Jo finite-n correction (bounded, model-free). Corpus: 200 Wikinews articles (2016 to 2021), 9 models, 5 prompting modes, 3 samples each. Uninstructed: 8 of 9 models significantly flatter than human; median paired gap in B_n 0.050 to 0.128; across 45 model-by-mode cells, 32 flatter, 9 no difference, 4 more dispersed. Under an explicit "vary your sentences" style instruction, 5 models still significantly flatter; none equivalent to human within +/-0.03. Few-shot human news exemplars made output significantly flatter than the instruction alone in every model. Markdown confound: raw instruction-mode output looked equivalent to human (B_n gap 0.006) until markdown was normalized away, after which it differed significantly (0.035, p = 2e-5), and the uninstructed gap grew from 0.053 to 0.102. — [NeurIPS 2026 listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/164820)
- A 2025 detection study operationalizes burstiness as the CV of sentence lengths (std/mean) with significance tests between original and generated text (per-model values not extracted). — [arXiv 2507.10475](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10475)
- Contrary view: newer, larger LLMs (GPT-3+) are "nearly indistinguishable" from human text statistically on the CT2 detectability index features. — [CT2, arXiv 2310.05030](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.05030)
- Markdown as fingerprint ("Idiosyncrasies in Large Language Models", Sun et al. 2025): a classifier on markdown elements alone identifies the source among ChatGPT/Claude/Grok/Gemini/DeepSeek at 73.1% (chat APIs) and 77.7% (instruct) vs 38.5% for base models (chance 20%), because base models mostly write plain text. Qualitatively: Claude very often uses no bold and no headers; ChatGPT bolds key points inside enumerations and uses headers; Gemini/Gemma use more italics. Full-text 5-way classification 97.1%; survives word shuffling (88.9%), paraphrase (91.4%), translation (91.8%); drops on summarization (58.1%). — [arXiv 2502.12150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12150)
- Reward pressure for formatting: Chatbot Arena style control regresses votes on answer length and counts of markdown headers, bold, and lists. Coefficients (control both): length 0.249, lists 0.031, headers 0.024, bold 0.019; a later LMArena refit: length 0.2381, headers 0.0290, lists 0.0201, bold 0.0135. All markdown coefficients are positive, meaning human voters prefer more formatting at fixed content. — [LMSYS style control](https://lmsys.org/blog/2024-08-28-style-control); [LMArena sentiment control](https://news.lmarena.ai/sentiment-control)
- Colon titles: Qwen 2.5 7B neutral-prompt scientific titles contained a colon 41.2% of the time vs 26.9% of published titles (Research Square preprint, few open models only). Pre-LLM bibliometric baseline: colons in 23% of highly cited and 18% of other paper titles; question-form titles 2%. — [Research Square rs-11137993](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11137993/v1); [arXiv 1804.10436](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.10436)
- A 2026 "temporal flattening" study finds average sentence length is the top single feature separating human from LLM writing trajectories (mean, not variance). — [arXiv 2604.12097](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.12097)

### Inferences
- New rule (not in slop-lint): **sentence-length dispersion**. Strip fenced code, headings, list items, tables, and blockquotes first (the Lanzo and Angelo markdown result says skipping this step hides the effect). Split sentences on `(?<=[.?!])\s+(?=["'(]?[A-Z])`, count words, require at least ~15 sentences. Compute CV = sd/mean. The only direct numbers (human ~0.78, ChatGPT ~0.50 in speeches) suggest a warn threshold near CV < 0.45 to 0.50 for prose paragraphs. To avoid the mean-length confound, either report CV (scale-free) rather than variance, or implement the Goh-Barabasi coefficient B = (sigma - mu)/(sigma + mu) on inter-boundary gaps, which is a few lines of arithmetic and zero-dependency. FP risk: medium. Technical docs, instructions, and legal text are uniformly written by humans too; short docs give unstable estimates.
- Burstiness should be advisory and paired with other signals: prompting can move it partway, and some models land within +/-0.05 of human, so it will miss many outputs; it helps most on long uninstructed prose.
- **Paragraph-length uniformity** can be computed the same way (CV of words per paragraph, at least 5 paragraphs). I found no published LLM-vs-human number; label it experimental.
- **Sentence-opening variety**: count the share of sentences whose first token is in a small set (This, The, It, These, By, As, While, With, In) or whose first two tokens repeat across adjacent sentences; also the fronted-participle opener from Q1. No published threshold found; experimental.
- **Formatting rules with evidence behind them**:
  - Bolded list lead-in, a ChatGPT signature per the Idiosyncrasies paper: `^\s*(?:[-*+]|\d+[.)])\s+\*\*[^*]{1,60}\*\*\s*[:.-]?` counted per list; warn when most items of a list start this way. FP risk: low to medium (some human style guides do this deliberately).
  - Colon headings: share of `^#{1,6}\s+[^:\n]{2,60}:\s+\S` among headings, or of title lines. 41.2% vs 26.9% suggests warn when more than ~40% of headings, and at least 3 headings, have the colon-reveal form. FP risk: medium (academic titles legitimately use colons at ~20 to 27%).
  - Heading/bullet counts per 1k words (R5.6 currently has no number): the Arena coefficients show reward pressure in that direction, but no human-baseline rate was found; calibrate on the specimen corpus.
- Model-family sensitivity: markdown-based rules will under-fire on Claude (often no bold, no headers) and over-fire on ChatGPT-style output; the linter catalogue should say so in the rule's source note.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed number for paragraph-length uniformity or sentence-opening variety in LLM vs human prose.
- No human baseline rates for headings, bold spans, or bullets per 1k words in comparable genres.
- Lanzo and Angelo is a workshop paper read at abstract level; model names and per-model B_n values were not visible.
- Muñoz-Ortiz et al. numbers (sentence-length SD, POS shares) could not be extracted from the PDF fetch.

## Q3. Slop taxonomies and structural molds: tricolons, colon-reveal headlines, "Here's the thing", rhetorical questions answered immediately, "So," openers, signposting, "Not because X. Because Y.", fragment staccato, false ranges, elegant variation, copula avoidance, "ensuring" tails. What is measured?

### Takeaway
Measured evidence exists for only a few of these: tricolon (about 1.9x per document and about 1.7x per sentence vs expert human argumentative prose, across GPT-5.1, DeepSeek-V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro), participial "ensuring" tails (via the 5.3x participle rate, Q1), syntactic templating (76% of LLM POS templates found in pretraining data vs 35% for human text), and colon titles (Q2). Rhetorical questions run the other way: humans used assertive rhetorical questions at more than twice the LLM rate, so a generic question-density rule would be wrong. The other listed molds have only practitioner or anecdotal support.

### Cited Findings
- Shaib et al., "Measuring AI 'Slop' in Text" (2025): expert-interview taxonomy with three themes. Information Utility: Density, Relevance. Information Quality: Factuality, Bias. Style Quality: Repetition, Templatedness, Coherence, Fluency, Verbosity, Word Complexity, Tone. All seven style/utility codes were significant positive predictors of a binary "slop" label; strongest were Relevance (beta 0.06), Density (0.05), Tone (0.05). Span count correlated with slop label (Spearman rho 0.70 news, 0.51 MS MARCO QA). — [arXiv 2509.19163](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v3)
- Same paper: binary slop agreement between annotators was poor (Cohen's kappa -0.12 to 0.11); among automatic methods, syntax-only features did best (AUROC 0.64 logistic regression, 0.68 PCA+LDA); LLM judges (GPT-5, DeepSeek-V3, o3-mini) had kappa near 0 with humans, flagged 3 to 8% of items as slop vs about 34% for humans, and GPT-5 span extraction precision/recall was about 0.08 to 0.19. — [arXiv 2509.19163](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v3)
- Shaib et al., "Detection and measurement of syntactic templates in generated text" (EMNLP 2024): models produce templated text at a higher rate than human references; about 76% of templates in model text occur in pretraining data vs about 35% for human text; templates are not removed by RLHF-style fine-tuning and can distinguish models, tasks, and domains. — [arXiv 2407.00211](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00211)
- Bakhshi, "Saying More Than They Know" (2026 preprint): 225 argumentative texts (~0.6M tokens), 75 each from human experts (Aeon, New Yorker, Guardian, Wired, etc.), human non-experts (Medium, Real Life), and LLMs (GPT-5.1 n=31, DeepSeek-V3.2 n=19, Claude Sonnet 4.6 n=13, Gemini 2.5 Pro n=12; 1,500-word target). Mean per document: tricolon 3.73 (expert), 4.87 (non-expert), 7.13 (LLM), effect 0.95, p < 0.001; erotema (assertive rhetorical question) 5.55 / 5.11 / 2.28; correctio 0.40 / 0.45 / 0.17; "performed" epistemic markers per sentence 0.057 / 0.058 / 0.114; "complexity tokens" 4.63 / 4.77 / 7.33. Mean sentences per document 92.8 / 96.7 / 104.7. Annotation was by LLM with no reported inter-annotator reliability. — [arXiv 2604.19768](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19768)
- Herbold et al. (2023): fewer discourse markers and epistemic markers in ChatGPT essays than in student essays. — [arXiv 2304.14276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14276v1)
- Tricolon described qualitatively ("lists of three successive elements") as a ChatGPT persuasion device. — [arXiv 2508.09614](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.09614v1)
- Rhetorical questions in clusters of three described as an AI habit (practitioner blog, no counts; anecdote, and contradicted by Bakhshi's erotema result). — [Cherryleaf 2026](https://www.cherryleaf.com/2026/02/indicators-that-suggest-something-was-written-by-ai/)
- Antislop (Paech et al., ICLR 2026): beyond the "not X, but Y" family (Q4), the over-represented items are mostly lexical and n-gram (sensory cliches, stock descriptors, intensifiers, character names) in creative writing; em-dashes were not analyzed as a standalone pattern. — [arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15061v1)

### Inferences
- The Shaib 2025 result is the strongest argument for slop-lint's approach: syntax-level features beat LLM judges, but the ceiling is modest (AUROC 0.64 to 0.68), so structural rules should stay warnings and be combined into a score, not used as verdicts.
- **Tricolon (R5.1, now has a number).** Per-sentence rates from Bakhshi: LLM 7.13/104.7 = 0.068; expert human 3.73/92.8 = 0.040; ratio about 1.7x. With the 1,500-word target, the LLM rate is roughly 4.5 to 5 per 1k words (derived, approximate). Regex sketch for noun/adjective triples: `\b(\w+(?:\s\w+){0,2}),\s+(\w+(?:\s\w+){0,2}),?\s+(?:and|or)\s+(\w+(?:\s\w+){0,2})\b` per 1k words; for clause triples, three consecutive sentences or clauses opening with the same word (anaphoric triad). Warn threshold around 1.5 to 2x the repo's human baseline. FP risk: high for genuine enumerations (ingredients, file lists, names). Exclude lines in lists/tables and triples containing digits, code spans, or capitalized proper nouns.
- **Do not add a rhetorical-question density rule.** Humans used more. Keep only narrow Q-then-answer molds (e.g., `(^|[.!]\s+)(?:The|So|And) [^?]{1,40}\?\s+[A-Z][^.?!]{1,40}[.!]` or the "the X? Y." form), labelled anecdotal.
- **Correctio** (self-correction, "or rather", "well, actually") was lower in LLM text; this is a different device from the negative parallelism in Q4 and does not undercut R4.
- **"Performed" hedging is double the human rate** (0.114 vs 0.057 per sentence) while Herbold finds fewer discourse/epistemic markers overall: LLMs swap genuine epistemic markers for performative ones ("it's worth noting", "arguably", "in many ways"). The existing HEDGE phrase list is aligned with this; a rate rule (hedge phrases per sentence > ~0.1) would be a calibrated extension.
- **Syntactic templates without a tagger.** POS tagging is not zero-dependency, but a function-word skeleton is: map each token to itself if it is in a ~150-word closed-class list, else to `W` (or `Wing`, `Wed`, `Wly` by suffix). Count 5- to 6-gram skeletons that repeat 3+ times in one document. This approximates Shaib's templates and would catch repeated molds without enumerating them. FP risk: medium; boilerplate docs (API references) are templated by design.
- **Copula avoidance ("serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "acts as" + a/an/the).** No quantified study found, but it is cheap and narrow: `\b(serves?|stands?|functions?|acts?)\s+as\s+(an?|the)\b`, per 1k words, warn on 2+ per document. FP risk: low-medium ("acts as a proxy" is literal in technical writing). slop-lint only catches "serves as a powerful/valuable/vital tool" and "stands/serves as a testament".
- **Participial "ensuring/highlighting/reflecting/underscoring" tail (R5.2)** is an instance of the 5.3x present-participle finding; a narrow regex `,\s+(ensuring|highlighting|reflecting|underscoring|emphasizing|showcasing|making|allowing|enabling|creating|providing|offering|fostering|paving)\b[^.?!]{3,}[.!]$` at sentence end is low-FP and evidence-backed.
- **False ranges ("from X to Y")**: no study found; regex `\bfrom [\w\s-]{2,30} to [\w\s-]{2,30}(,| and)` (stacked ranges) would be practitioner-grade only.
- **Elegant variation / synonym cycling (R5.5)**: no measured study found; not regex-tractable without coreference.

### Gaps
- No quantified study found for: colon-reveal headlines in prose headings (only scientific titles, one open model), "Here's the thing", "So," sentence openers, signposting density, "Not because X. Because Y.", fragment staccato, false ranges, elegant variation, copula avoidance. These remain practitioner-catalogue items (e.g., Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" essay, which slop-lint already cites) and should be labelled anecdotal.
- Bakhshi's counts are LLM-annotated with no reliability check and a small corpus (75 per group); treat the 1.7 to 1.9x tricolon ratio as a first estimate.
- No per-1k-word rates in Bakhshi; my per-1k conversion assumes the 1,500-word target was met.

## Q4. Negative parallelism ("It's not about X. It's about Y.", "not X but Y"): measured prevalence in 2025 to 2026 models?

### Takeaway
The only quantified research figure I found is from Antislop (ICLR 2026): the "not X, but Y" family is reported at about 6.3x the human rate for some open models in creative writing, and a regex ban drives it to zero without hurting benchmarks. Everything else is journalism or blog numbers, some secondhand. One rhetoric study found the related device "correctio" less common in LLM prose, which is a definitional difference, not a contradiction.

### Cited Findings
- Antislop (Paech, Roush, Goldfeder, Shwartz-Ziv; ICLR 2026): "It's not X, it's Y" constructions reported as 6.3x more prevalent than in human writing for some models; detection via five regexes (Appendix L) matching a negation ("not", "n't", "no longer") then a clause break then "it/they/you" + "is/was/are", or a dash/comma + "but", with negative lookaheads to drop false positives such as "not only ... time". Per-1k-character rates by model are only in a figure. Baseline: wordfreq for words; Reddit creative writing plus Project Gutenberg for n-grams. Models studied include gemma-3-12b (primary), Mistral-Small-3.2, Llama-3.3-70B, across 67 models for the lexical fingerprint. Regex-enforced qwen3-4b reached 0.00 hits; FTPO fine-tuning cut slop about 90% while holding GSM8K/MMLU/creative-writing scores. — [arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15061v1); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10008156)
- Corporate writing (secondhand): a Barron's count of the construction in AlphaSense-indexed press releases, earnings reports, and filings reportedly rose from about 50 uses in 2023 to more than 200 in 2025. — [thewritingking.com summary](https://thewritingking.com/its-not-x-its-y-ai-tell/) (original Barron's analysis not retrieved)
- Unsourced "roughly three times more often in machine output" estimate. — [After the Update substack](https://aftertheupdate.substack.com/p/every-ai-learned-the-same-sentence) (anecdote; source of the 3x not identified)
- An OpenAI product manager called the pattern "contrastive phrasing"; explanations offered include reward signals and training on AI-contaminated text. — [completeaitraining.com](https://completeaitraining.com/news/the-ai-writing-tic-its-not-x-its-y-persists-as-chatbots/) (journalism, secondhand)
- The construction predates chatbots by centuries (argument against treating any single instance as decisive). — [brandonlazovic.dev](https://brandonlazovic.dev/articles/llm-negative-parallelism-tic/)
- Correctio (self-correcting a statement) was rarer in LLM argumentative prose: 0.17 vs 0.40 per document for expert humans (GPT-5.1, DeepSeek-V3.2, Claude Sonnet 4.6, Gemini 2.5 Pro). — [arXiv 2604.19768](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19768)

### Inferences
- slop-lint's R4.1 to R4.5 regexes are more specific than Antislop's Appendix L family in some ways (noun-subject, uncontracted two-sentence forms) and narrower in others. Two Antislop elements are worth porting: (a) "no longer" as a negation trigger (covers the pending "is no longer X. It is Y." mold), and (b) "you" as a valid second-clause subject ("You're not X. You're Y.", common in reply/coaching register). Sketch: `\b(?:it|this|that|they|you|we)(?:'s|'re| is| are| was| were)\s+(?:not|no longer|never)\b[^.?!]{1,80}[.,;:]\s*(?:it|this|that|they|you|we)(?:'s|'re| is| are| was| were)\b`.
- The "It's not about X. It's about Y." variant is caught by R4.2 only when both halves sit on one line; since slop-lint is line-based, a two-line split (common in staccato markdown) escapes. Running PHRASES on a paragraph-joined buffer would fix this.
- Because the pattern is old and human, a per-document count (warn at 2+ per 1k words, or 1 in a reply under 300 words) is better calibrated than flagging each instance. 6.3x over a human rate means single occurrences are still mostly human-plausible.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed per-1k-word prevalence for negative parallelism in GPT-5.x, Claude 4.x/5.x, or Gemini 3.x non-fiction output was found. The Antislop number is from creative writing and mostly open models.
- The Barron's 2023 to 2025 corporate-filing counts and the 3x ratio could not be verified at the original source.

## Q5. Dialogue/register tells: sycophancy openers, closing offers, second-person overuse, uniform warmth

### Takeaway
Social sycophancy is well measured: across 8 models, LLMs offered emotional validation in 76% of advice cases vs 22% for humans and used indirect language 87% vs 20%. Opening/closing formula words are model fingerprints (ChatGPT "certainly", "below is"; Claude "here", "based on"; Gemma "let me", "know if"). I found no study counting closing offers ("Let me know if...") or second-person rates directly.

### Cited Findings
- ELEPHANT (Cheng et al., Stanford/CMU/Oxford; ICLR 2026): v1 (8 models) reports LLMs offer emotional validation in 76% of cases vs 22% for humans, use indirect language 87% vs 20%, preserve face 47% more than humans on open-ended questions, and affirm behavior crowds judged inappropriate in 42% of r/AITA cases. v2 (11 models) reports a 45-percentage-point gap over humans on general advice queries and that on moral conflicts LLMs affirm whichever side the user takes in 48% of cases. — [arXiv 2505.13995 v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13995v1); [v2 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13995v2); [ICLR 2026 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2026/poster/10007944)
- First-word and characteristic-word fingerprints: ChatGPT favors "such as", "certainly", "overall" and often begins "certainly" or "below is"; Claude favors "here", "according to", "based on" and references the prompt ("based on the text"); Llama favors "including", "such as"; Gemma favors "let me" and "know if" (closing-offer fragments). Claude uses "the/and/to/of" far less than other chat APIs. — [Idiosyncrasies in LLMs, arXiv 2502.12150](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12150)
- LMArena added a sentiment-control term alongside style control, i.e., tone/positivity is a measurable confound in human preference votes (details of sentiment coefficients not extracted). — [LMArena sentiment control](https://news.lmarena.ai/sentiment-control)
- "Tone" is one of the three strongest predictors of a human "slop" label (beta 0.05). — [arXiv 2509.19163](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19163v3)

### Inferences
- ELEPHANT's 76% vs 22% validation rate supports R5.10/R5.11 and the validate-then-restate PHRASES as high-base-rate tells in advice/reply register. Additional opener regexes (line 1 of a reply, or first sentence): `^(?:certainly|absolutely|of course|sure|great question|good question)\b[!,.]`, `^below is\b`, `^(?:it'?s|that'?s) (?:completely |totally |perfectly )?(?:understandable|normal|valid|okay)\b`, `^that sounds (?:really |so )?(?:hard|difficult|frustrating|exciting|stressful)\b`. FP risk: low in documents, medium in genuine chat (people do say "Sure,").
- Closing offers (R5.13) have fingerprint support (Gemma "let me ... know if") but no prevalence count. A last-paragraph-only regex is low-FP: `\b(?:let me know if|feel free to|happy to (?:help|dig|expand)|would you like me to|if you'd like, i can|want me to)\b` restricted to the final 2 sentences.
- Indirect language at 87% vs 20% suggests a rate rule for softeners in replies ("you might consider", "it may be worth", "one option could be") per sentence, but no lexicon from the paper was retrieved; experimental.
- Second-person overuse and "uniform warmth" have no direct measurement I could find; the Biber D1 finding (LLM prose shifts away from involved features) predicts that in essay/blog prose LLMs use second person less than humans, while in chat they use templated second person ("you're right that", "what you're describing"). Treat second-person density as register-dependent and do not add a global rule.

### Gaps
- No study counting closing-offer frequency, "Great question" openers, or second-person pronoun rates in assistant replies vs human replies was found.
- ELEPHANT's human 22% baseline is from v1 only; v2 framing changed. Check v2 before quoting.
- No measurement of "uniform warmth" (low variance of sentiment across a reply or across replies) was found, though LMArena's sentiment control indicates it is measurable with a sentiment lexicon.
