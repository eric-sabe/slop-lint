# The craft of academic and scientific prose: principles and testable checks for the serious-academic skill

Scope note. The skill under review is /Users/eric/.claude/skills/serious-academic/SKILL.md (twelve commandments). The skill already covers: a cold reader, the point in the first sentence of a paragraph, connected sentences plus a swap test, problem before mechanism, no unasked rebuttals, one name per thing, no change history, cutting what the reader does not need, one idea per sentence plus a 40-word cap, the meaning of each number, a concrete case before a scheme, and claims sized to evidence. The notes below report only what is new or what gives an existing commandment a sharper test. Each candidate is tagged NEW or SHARPENS-n (n = commandment number).

Source quality. Gopen & Swan (1990) and Mensh & Kording (2017) were read in full from primary text. Hayot's five levels were taken from a blog that quotes p. 60 of the book verbatim. Williams, Pinker, Sword, Hyland, Booth et al., Zinsser and Pullum were reached through secondary summaries, writing-center handouts and search snippets, because the books are not online. Those items are flagged, and exact wording should be checked against the books before it is quoted in the skill.

## 1. Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (American Scientist 78, 1990): turning reader-expectation principles into tests

### Takeaway
Gopen & Swan give seven structural principles: subject next to verb, new information in the stress position, the story's character and the backward-linking old information in the topic position, the action in the verb, and context before anything new. Each one can be checked mechanically on a draft. They also contradict two of the skill's current tests: the fixed 40-word sentence cap and treating every semicolon as a defect.

### Cited Findings
- The seven principles, verbatim: "1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb. 2. Place in the stress position the 'new information' you want the reader to emphasize. 3. Place the person or thing whose 'story' a sentence is telling at the beginning of the sentence, in the topic position. 4. Place appropriate 'old information' (material already stated in the discourse) in the topic position for linkage backward and contextualization forward. 5. Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb. 6. In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new. 7. In general, try to ensure that the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the relative expectations for emphasis raised by the structure." — [Gopen & Swan 1990, final section "Writing and the Scientific Process"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- The authors call these principles, not rules: "Slavish adherence to them will succeed no better than has slavish adherence to avoiding split infinitives or to using the active voice instead of the passive." — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Subject-verb separation: "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance." In their first example, 23 words separate the subject from its verb, and in another sentence 27 words do. — [Gopen & Swan 1990, "Subject-Verb Separation"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Stress position: readers emphasize what comes at the end of a sentence. If the writer puts unemphatic material there, the reader may "emphasize this imposter material". — [Gopen & Swan 1990, "The Stress Position"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Sentence length. They reject word-count limits ("the favorite is 29") and offer this definition instead: "A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available." — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Semicolons: "By using a semicolon, we created a second stress position to accommodate a second piece of [emphasis-worthy information]". A properly used colon or semicolon creates a secondary stress position. — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- They give the principle in a guarded form: "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize." They also call misplaced old and new information "the No. 1 problem in American professional writing today". The cause they name is that writers rush to record the new thought first and add the linking context afterward. — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Topic-position diagnostic (earthquake example). They list the opening words of each sentence ("Large earthquakes / The rates / Therefore... one / subsequent mainshocks / great plate boundary ruptures / the southern segment of the San Andreas fault / the smaller the standard deviation...") and show that most of them are new information. As a result, "no two readers would be likely to construct exactly the same story." The recurring concept ("recurrence intervals") "rarely appears at the beginning of sentences." — [Gopen & Swan 1990, "The Topic Position"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Logical gaps. "When old information does not appear at all in a sentence... readers are left to construct the logical linkage by themselves." In their nucleoside example the stress position of sentence 2 promises two effects, and sentence 3 never mentions them. Fixing the structure forced the authors to add two missing sentences of reasoning. — [Gopen & Swan 1990, "Perceiving Logical Gaps"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Before/after (nucleoside, revised): "We have directly measured the enthalpy of hydrogen bond formation between the nucleoside bases 2'deoxyguanosine (dG) and 2'deoxycytidine (dC). dG and dC were derivatized at the 5' and 3' hydroxyls with triisopropylsilyl groups; these groups serve both to solubilize the nucleosides in non-aqueous solvents and to prevent the ribose hydroxyls from forming hydrogen bonds. Consequently, when the derivatized nucleosides are dissolved in non-aqueous solvents, hydrogen bonds form almost exclusively between the bases. Since the interbase hydrogen bonds are the only bonds to form upon mixing, their enthalpy of formation can be determined directly by measuring the enthalpy of mixing. From our isoperibolic titration measurements, the enthalpy of dC:dG base pair formation is -6.65+0.32 kcal/mol." — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Action in the verb (egg-extract example). They list the verbs of the original paragraph ("is / is ... is / are presumed to be / are transcribed / has / is ... can be alleviated / destabilizes") and conclude that the list "gives too few clues as to what actions actually take place." The real actions, "limit" and "inhibit", never appear as verbs. Original opening: "Transcription of the 5S RNA genes in the egg extract is TFIIIA-dependent. This is surprising, because the concentration of TFIIIA is the same as in the oocyte nuclear extract." Revised: "In the egg extract, the availability of TFIIIA limits transcription of the 5S RNA genes. This is surprising because the same concentration of TFIIIA does not limit transcription in the oocyte nuclear extract." — [Gopen & Swan 1990, "Locating the Action"](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Structure and thought: in three of their four examples, revising the structure "revealed existing conceptual gaps" that could be filled only by adding material or cutting it. "The structure of the prose becomes the structure of the scientific argument." — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)

### Inferences (candidate skill text)
- **SHARPENS-3. Topic-string test** (replaces or supplements the swap test). For each paragraph, list the first five words of every sentence (the topic position). Pass: most entries name something the reader has already met, usually the paragraph's main character, or they pick up the previous sentence's ending. Fail: most entries are first mentions, or the character changes in every sentence. Fix: move the backward-linking item to the front and push the new item to the end.
- **SHARPENS-3. Promise test.** Read the last few words of each sentence (the stress position). If they introduce something new, the next sentence or two must say what happens to it. Fail: the stress position introduces an item that never appears again, as with Gopen & Swan's "two effects". That pattern marks a missing step of reasoning, so add the step. Do not simply reorder.
- **NEW. Stress-position audit.** List the final words of each sentence. Fail if a sentence ends on a citation, a hedge ("in some cases", "to some extent", "as discussed above"), a date, a method detail, or a qualifying clause while its claim sits earlier. Fix: move the claim to the end and the qualifier to the front or into a separate sentence. (An inference from Principle 2 and the "imposter material" passage. LLM drafts often end sentences with trailing qualifiers and parenthetical citations.)
- **NEW. Subject-verb gap.** Count the words between each main-clause subject head and its verb. Fail if any gap is more than about 7 words. The 7-word figure comes from the BU handout below, not from Gopen & Swan, who give no number. Fix by making the intervening material its own sentence or a following clause.
- **NEW / SHARPENS-9. Verb-list test.** List the main verbs of a paragraph. Fail if most are forms of be or have, "show", "provide", "involve", "remain", or passive "is presumed to be". Fix: find the real action, which is often hiding in a noun (limitation → limit), and make it the verb.
- **SHARPENS-9. Replace the fixed 40-word cap with Gopen & Swan's stress-position definition.** A sentence is too long when it holds more emphasis-worthy items than it has stress positions (one per sentence, plus one per correctly used semicolon or colon). Keep 40 words only as a trigger to look again. The skill's semicolon count should flag semicolons that join unrelated facts, not semicolons that create a second stress position for a related point (the revised nucleoside passage uses one).

### Gaps
- Gopen & Swan cite no controlled experiments. Their evidence is worked revision plus the reader-expectation literature, which they credit to Williams and to Colomb & Williams (1985). I found no empirical test of the stress-position claim specifically.

## 2. Williams, Pinker, Sword (Writer's Diet), Hayot, Booth/Colomb/Williams, Mensh & Kording, Zinsser, Strunk & White critiques

### Takeaway
Nine items are missing from the skill. From Williams: put characters in subjects and actions in verbs, keep a stable topic string, and treat issue and point. From Pinker: avoid metadiscourse about the text itself and compulsive hedging. From Sword: a word-class audit with numeric thresholds. From Hayot: paragraphs that move between abstraction levels and end higher than they start. From Mensh & Kording: one central contribution, no zig-zag, declarative headers and figure titles that state a conclusion. Two sources conflict with the skill as written: Mensh & Kording and Hayot both put a conclusion in the last sentence of a paragraph, and Pinker attacks signposting that the skill's Register section encourages.

### Cited Findings
**Williams (Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace; Williams & Bizup in later editions).** No full text was available; these points come from handouts.
- Three principles, from a Yale handout adapted from Williams & Bizup, Style 5th ed.: make main characters subjects, make important actions verbs, avoid nominalizations when possible. — [Yale-NUS handout listing via search](https://yale-nus.edu.sg/academics/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2021/12/Grammar-Style-Actions-Characters.pdf)
- Diagnostic from a BU module built on Williams: check whether "a concrete subject and a specific verb" arrive "within the first 6-7 words". Highlight all verbs and underline their subjects. Ask whether the subjects are people or abstract nouns, and whether people appear in the sentence without being subjects. Highlight every "to be" or "to have" verb and underline the nominalizations ("argument," "understanding," "analysis," "suggestion"). Then ask who would perform each action if the noun became a verb. — [BU, Sentence Clarity: Characters and Actions](https://www.bu.edu/teaching-writing/resources/sentence-clarity-characters-and-actions)
- Nominalization repair: when an empty verb ("conducted") takes a nominalization as its object, turn the nominalization into the verb. — [Williams excerpt via search, Michigan Bar Journal plain-English column](https://www.michbar.org/file/generalinfo/plainenglish/pdfs/92_feb.pdf)
- Cohesion and coherence. Cohesion is the connection between adjacent sentences. Coherence is the sense that all the parts add up. — [UMN Writing Across the Curriculum](https://wac.umn.edu/tww-program/teaching-writing-blog/helping-students-improve-cohesion)
- Topic strings: keep a small, consistent set of topics per paragraph and "avoid random shifts made only for variety". Test: underline the first few words of each sentence to check that they share one topic. — [GVSU handout](https://www.gvsu.edu/cms5/asset/34fe3426-20ed-4067-b41e-84ebdafe2999/0e202a1a-fe9c-4040-9d84-3c4f2c591a1c/organization_within_paragraphs.pdf); [summary of Williams & Bizup](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup.md)
- Issue and point: the sentences of a paragraph develop a topic toward a clear point, and the point "need not appear in the first sentence." Williams allows more than one position for it. — [summary of Williams & Bizup, 13th ed.](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup.md)
- The passive is acceptable when it serves cohesion or emphasis. The question is whether it hides an actor the reader needs to know. — [summary of Williams & Bizup](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-joseph-m-williams-joseph-bizup.md)

**Pinker (The Sense of Style, 2014; "Why Academics Stink at Writing," Chronicle Review, 2014).**
- The curse of knowledge, "the failure to understand that other people don't know what we know," is the main cause of bad writing. — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-curse-of-knowledge-pinker-describes-a-key-cause-of-bad-writing); [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge)
- The Chronicle essay lists the symptoms of academese: metadiscourse, professional narcissism, apologizing, shudder quotes, hedging, metaconcepts, and nominalizations. — [Harvard Pamphlet blog summary](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/pamphlet/2014/09/29/inaccessible-writing-in-both-senses-of-the-term/); [Chronicle (paywalled)](https://www.chronicle.com/article/why-academics-stink-at-writing/)
- Metadiscourse is "verbiage about verbiage" and arises from "agonizing self-consciousness". Pinker says it helps the writer, not the reader. — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/fixing-psychology/201410/why-academic-writing-sucks)
- "Compulsive hedging", signaled by "somewhat, comparatively, and to a certain degree", which Pinker attributes to a need to cover oneself. He accepts some hedging but says skilled writers hedge sparingly. — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/fixing-psychology/201410/why-academic-writing-sucks)
- Classic style is defined most basically as "seeing the world": the writer directs the reader's attention to something in the world rather than to the writer's own process. — [search summary of Sense of Style reviews](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/virtues-grammar-science-ethics-style/)
- Counterpoint: Stuart Shieber argues that roadmap signposting helps a reader grasp the high-level structure of a long article. — [Harvard Pamphlet](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/pamphlet/2014/09/29/inaccessible-writing-in-both-senses-of-the-term/)

**Sword (Stylish Academic Writing, Harvard UP 2012; The Writer's Diet, 2007/2016).**
- In her analysis of 1,000 articles (100 from each of ten disciplines), fewer than 10% met all three of her criteria: engagement, accessibility and craft. Many had "dull, passive prose" and "multisyllabic, abstract nouns". Other reports put the sample at 500 for some criteria, so the sample size conflicts across sources. — [LSE blog](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/politicsandpolicy/academic-success-stylish-writing-sword); [THE](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/my-readers-i-buried-them/419748.article)
- The Writer's Diet test has five categories: be-verbs, zombie nouns (nominalizations), prepositions, ad-words (adjectives and adverbs), and "it, this, that, there". For nouns, 5% nominalizations scores "Needs toning", 6% "Flabby", and 7% or more "Heart attack" (checked against the user guide 2026-11-25). — [Sword, Writer's Diet user guide](https://helensword.com/writersdiet-user-guide); [Auckland UP sample](https://cdn.auckland.ac.nz/assets/press/all-books/pdfs/2015/Sword_the-writers-diet_blad.pdf)
- Sword's own caveat: the test is "an automated feedback tool, not an assessment tool" and is "not designed to judge the overall quality of your writing". A stylish text can score badly. — [writersdiet.com](https://writersdiet.com/writing-test/)

**Hayot (The Elements of Academic Style, Columbia UP 2014, ch. 8 "The Uneven U," p. 59-60).**
- Five levels, quoted from p. 60. Level 5: "Abstract; general, oriented toward a solution or conclusion." Level 4: "Less general; orientated toward a problem; pulls ideas together." Level 3: "Conceptual summary; draws together two or more pieces of evidence, or introduces a broad example." Level 2: "Description; plain or interpretive summary; establishing shot." Level 1: "Concrete; evidentiary; raw; unmediated data or information." — [Thesis Whisperer, quoting Hayot p. 60](https://thesiswhisperer.com/2019/02/13/the-uneven-u/)
- Shape: open at level 4, descend toward level 1, close at level 5. Save the sweeping statement for the last sentence rather than the first. Diagnostic: label each sentence by level; a paragraph that "hovers around level three" may lack substance. — [Thesis Whisperer](https://thesiswhisperer.com/2019/02/13/the-uneven-u/); [University Affairs](https://universityaffairs.ca/career-advice/crafting-a-strong-conclusion-to-your-journal-article-or-book-chapter/)

**Mensh & Kording, "Ten simple rules for structuring papers," PLOS Comput Biol 13(9): e1005619 (2017).**
- Rule 1: one central contribution, stated in the title. Readers should still be able to describe it a year later. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 2: write for a reader who does not know the work. "Avoid abbreviations and acronyms so that readers do not have to go back." Limit the number of open threads the reader must hold. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 3: context-content-conclusion at the scale of the paper, the paragraph and the abstract. In a paragraph, "the first sentence defines the topic or context," the body adds new content, and the last sentence gives the conclusion. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 4: avoid zig-zag, so each subject is covered in one place. Use parallel syntax for parallel points. "Resist the temptation to use a different word to refer to the same concept." — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 5: the abstract runs broad, narrow, broad: context, the gap, "Here we", results, and a conclusion that answers the question. Do not present results "before the reader is ready to understand them." — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 6: in the introduction, paragraphs become progressively more specific and end with the gap. The introduction "should not contain a broad literature review beyond the motivation of the paper." — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 7: results subsection headers are declarative statements. Each paragraph poses its question first and ends with the answer. "The title of the figure should communicate the conclusion of the analysis." — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 8: the first paragraph of the discussion summarizes the findings. Later paragraphs take strengths and weaknesses, each linked to the literature, and the section ends with how the work moves the field. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)
- Rule 10: if you cannot summarize the paper's outline to a colleague in a few minutes, readers will struggle. Vague reviewer criticism often means the reader missed the big-picture story. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)

**Zinsser (On Writing Well).**
- "Clutter is the disease of American writing. We are a society strangling in unnecessary words, circular constructions, pompous frills and meaningless jargon." He brackets "the little qualifiers that weaken any sentence they inhabit ('a bit,' 'sort of'), or phrases like 'in a sense,' which don't mean anything." — [Macmillan reading excerpt, "Clutter"](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/readwrite/readwrite_ch52_26.html); [LitCharts ch. 2](https://www.litcharts.com/lit/on-writing-well/chapter-2-simplicity)

**Strunk & White critiques.**
- Pullum argues that three of the four "Use the active voice" examples in The Elements of Style are not passives. Strunk & White themselves say the passive is "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary." — [Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=1318); [Chronicle, "50 Years of Stupid Grammar Advice"](https://chronicle.com/article/50-Years-of-Stupid-Grammar/25497)

### Inferences (candidate skill text)
- **NEW. Characters and actions.** For each sentence, name the character (who or what acts) and the action. Pass: the character is the grammatical subject and the action is the main verb. Fail: the subject is an abstraction ("the implementation of", "the utilization of") or the verb is empty ("perform", "conduct", "provide", "make", be or have plus a nominalization). Example (from the skill's own domain, not a source): "An analysis of the traffic was performed by the instrumentation" becomes "The instrumentation analyzed the traffic." Exception: keep the passive when it puts old information in the topic position (Williams, Gopen & Swan). Do not add a passive ban (Pullum).
- **NEW. Nominalization density** (Sword's threshold). Count words ending in -tion, -ment, -ness, -ity, -ance or -ence, -ism, -al (as in "removal") and -ing used as a noun. Flag a paragraph where they make up 5% or more of the words, or where one sentence has three or more. Keep a nominalization that names a defined term the paper counts with (commandment 6) or that refers back to a preceding sentence ("This limitation" as old information).
- **NEW. Bare demonstratives.** Writer's Diet scores "it, this, that, there" as a category. Fail: a sentence starts with "This", "That", "These" or "It" with no noun after it, or with "There is" or "There are". Fix: "This" plus a noun that names what is referred to ("This undercount", "These 145 apps"). The this-plus-noun repair is a common writing-center rule; I did not find it in Sword's own text.
- **SHARPENS-2. Point position.** The skill requires the point in the first sentence. Mensh & Kording and Hayot both also want the last sentence to deliver the conclusion, and Williams allows the point at the end of a short opening issue. Proposed test: the first sentence states the paragraph's question or claim at Hayot level 4 (specific, problem-oriented, not a sweeping generality). The last sentence states what the paragraph established at level 5, not a bare datum, citation or hedge. Fail: the last sentence is a level-1 or level-2 detail, so the paragraph trails off. Fail: the first sentence is a generality that would fit any paper in the field ("X has received increasing attention").
- **NEW. Abstraction-level map** (Hayot). Label each sentence 1 to 5. Fail: every sentence is 3 or higher, meaning no concrete evidence and only summary of summary. Fail: every sentence is 1 or 2, a list of facts with no point. This test makes commandment 11 (concrete case before scheme) apply at paragraph scale and catches the "list of facts" failure the professor named.
- **NEW. No zig-zag** (Mensh & Kording Rule 4). For each main object or topic, list the paragraphs that discuss it. Fail: a topic appears in non-adjacent paragraphs of one section without a reason that is stated.
- **NEW. Declarative headers and figure titles** (Mensh & Kording Rule 7). Fail: a results subsection header or figure title that names a topic ("Network traffic analysis") instead of stating a finding ("Most flows leave through third-party SDKs").
- **NEW. Talk about the world, not the text** (Pinker's metadiscourse). Fail: sentences whose subject is the paper, section or authors' plan ("In this section, we will discuss", "As mentioned above", "It is worth noting that", "It is important to highlight"). Keep: a single roadmap sentence at the end of a long introduction (Shieber's counterpoint), and ordinal signposts that organize content ("First, ... Second, ..."), which the skill already prescribes. This resolves the conflict with the skill's Register section: count signposts (allowed) and metadiscourse about the text (cut) separately.
- **SHARPENS-12. Qualifier sweep** (Zinsser, Pinker). Flag "somewhat", "comparatively", "to a certain degree", "in a sense", "relatively", "fairly", "arguably", "potentially", "may help to". Each one must name what it qualifies. Otherwise delete it, or replace it with the number or condition that bounds the claim ("in 12 of 30 runs").

### Gaps
- No full text of Williams's Style or Pinker's Sense of Style was available. I could not verify Williams's issue/point placement rules at the section and document level, his "shape of sentences" lesson (resumptive, summative and free modifiers; short before long), or Pinker's chapter on coherence relations (resemblance, contrast, cause, elaboration). The well-known Williams before/after examples (for example Little Red Riding Hood rewritten with nominalizations) are not quoted here because I could not retrieve them from a source.
- Writer's Diet thresholds for be-verbs, prepositions and ad-words were not found. Only the 5% nominalization threshold was confirmed.

## 3. Empirical evidence on readability, jargon, citations and hedging

### Takeaway
Scientific abstracts have become harder to read over more than a century, and the rise of general scientific jargon accounts for much of that. Heavy jargon in abstracts is associated with fewer citations, and narrative abstracts with more, but the broad readability-citation literature is mixed and the effects are small. Needless complex vocabulary lowers readers' judgments of the author's intelligence. Hedging data statements hurts persuasion, and stating limitations has at best a small, unreplicated benefit for trust. The defensible message for the skill: clarity costs nothing and sometimes helps. Do not tell the writer that clearer prose earns more citations.

### Cited Findings
- Plavén-Sigray, Matheson, Schiffler & Thompson (eLife 2017;6:e27725) analyzed 709,577 abstracts from 123 journals published 1881-2015. Flesch Reading Ease and New Dale-Chall both show readability falling steadily (the two measures correlate at r = -0.72). The trend comes with growing use of general scientific jargon ("science-specific common words"), and the decline extends to full texts. Reviewers asked the authors to use "fewer nouns and more verbs", and they did. — [eLife article](https://elifesciences.org/articles/27725); [eLife peer reviews](https://elifesciences.org/articles/27725/peer-reviews); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/119370v1.full.pdf+html)
- Martínez & Mammola (Proc R Soc B 2021, doi:10.1098/rspb.2020.2581) studied 21,486 cave-science articles and found that a higher share of jargon in titles and abstracts goes with fewer citations. None of the most highly cited papers used jargon in the title. Press coverage reports that citations dropped once jargon in the abstract exceeded about 1%; this threshold is secondary and unverified. The design is observational. — [PMC8059506](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8059506); [CBC As It Happens](https://www.cbc.ca/radio/asithappens/as-it-happens-tuesday-edition-1.5985611/say-what-more-jargon-in-a-paper-means-fewer-scientists-will-read-it-study-finds-1.5985613)
- Hillier, Kelly & Klinger (PLOS ONE 2016, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0167983) scored 732 climate-science abstracts for narrativity and found that more narrative abstracts were cited more often. Effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Altmetric record / abstract](https://www.altmetric.com/details/14777545)
- Conflicting evidence. Lu et al. (J. Informetrics 2019, 13(3):817-829) found no practically significant relation between full-text linguistic complexity and citations in biology and psychology. Lei & Yan (2016) found no correlation for information-science abstracts. Didegah & Thelwall (2013) found a weak link in biology only. Wang, Liu & Zhou (2022) found a significant but very small negative effect of complexity. Sienkiewicz & Altmann (2016) found a positive association between complexity and citations. — [search summary with links](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/scient/v108y2016i3d10.1007_s11192-016-2036-9.html); [Emerald OIR 2020 review](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/OIR-05-2020-0188/full/html)
- Oppenheimer (Applied Cognitive Psychology 2006, "Consequences of Erudite Vernacular Utilized Irrespective of Necessity") replaced words with longer synonyms in graduate admission essays, sociology dissertation abstracts and translations of Descartes. In Experiments 1-3, more complex texts led readers to judge the author less intelligent, whatever the essay's quality. The mechanism is processing fluency. A majority of undergraduates said they inflate vocabulary to sound intelligent. — [Princeton news via search](https://www.dailyprincetonian.com/article/2006/10/on-erudite-vernacular-i-e-big-words); [Wiley DOI 10.1002/acp.1178](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/acp.1178)
- Hedging and persuasion. Britt, Durik and colleagues (2008) report that hedges on data statements, and colloquial hedges on interpretation statements, can weaken persuasion. Full effect sizes were not retrieved. — [Benjamins listing](https://benjamins.com/online/ebop/publications/52503)
- Jensen (2008): news stories that hedged by stating a study's limitations raised the scientists' trustworthiness ratings. Ratcliff et al. (2018) failed to replicate this in a larger sample. Gustafson & Rice (2019) found that uncertainty frames usually have no significant effect, apart from small negative effects of uncertainty about consensus. — [Gustafson & Rice 2019](https://rrice.faculty.comm.ucsb.edu/A145GustafsonRice2019UncertaintyFramesScienceCommunicationTopics.pdf); [Health Promotion Canada 2025 review](https://www.canada.ca/content/dam/phac-aspc/documents/services/reports-publications/health-promotion-chronic-disease-prevention-canada-research-policy-practice/vol-45-no-10-2025/uncertainty-communication-trust-health-promotion.pdf)

### Inferences (candidate skill text)
- **SHARPENS-12. Hedge interpretations, not data.** Report a measured value without a hedge ("The pipeline flagged 145 apps", not "approximately 145 apps may have been flagged"). Place qualification on inference and generalization ("which suggests", "in this sample"). Test: list every hedge and mark whether it attaches to an observation or an interpretation. Fail: a hedge on an observation the writer measured. This follows the Britt/Durik finding. The Jensen result supports stating limitations explicitly, but it did not replicate, so the skill should cite it as weak evidence.
- **SHARPENS-6. Plain-word substitution** (Oppenheimer). Where a shorter common word has the same technical meaning ("use" for "utilize", "show" for "demonstrate", "method" for "methodology", "about" for "approximately" when the number is not exact), use it. This rule does not apply to field terms: commandment 6 already keeps the field's standard terms.
- **NEW, abstract and title only. Jargon budget** (Martínez & Mammola). Count terms in the title and abstract that a reader in an adjacent subfield would not know. Each must be defined in place or replaced. Keep the title jargon-free where the field allows it. Treat the reported ~1% threshold as unverified.
- Rationale text for the skill. Readability has declined for more than a century (Plavén-Sigray). The citation benefit of clarity is mixed and small (Lu et al.), so the case for clarity rests on comprehension and on how readers judge the author (Oppenheimer), not on citation counts.

## 4. Argument quality: contribution, positioning against prior work, limitations, falsifiable claims, and stance

### Takeaway
Strong academic prose commits. It names one central contribution and states the problem as a condition plus a cost to the reader. It makes claims supported by reasons, evidence and stated warrants. It acknowledges the objections a field reader would actually raise, and answers them, and no others. It uses self-mention and calibrated boosters to show where the writer stands. Hyland's stance and engagement model gives a vocabulary for auditing this. The Craft of Research gives a rule for which objections to acknowledge, and that rule sharpens commandment 5 rather than contradicting it.

### Cited Findings
- Booth, Colomb & Williams, The Craft of Research. Topic → question → significance: "I am studying ... because I want to find out what/why/how ... to help my reader understand ...". A problem has a condition and a cost, and "Readers judge the significance of your problem not by the cost you pay", so the cost must be framed from the reader's side. Practical problems concern action; conceptual problems concern understanding. — [LessWrong notes on Craft of Research, ch. 4](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fvJECtgMX4cP62t2s/cliffnotes-to-craft-of-research-parts-i-ii-and-iii)
- An argument has five parts: claim, reasons, evidence, acknowledgment and response, and warrant. The warrant answers "How does that follow?" and is often left implicit. — [LessWrong notes, ch. 7](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fvJECtgMX4cP62t2s/cliffnotes-to-craft-of-research-parts-i-ii-and-iii)
- Which objections to acknowledge (ch. 10): plausible charges of weakness, alternative arguments that matter in the field, alternative conclusions readers want to be true, and counterexamples. Readers' challenges are intrinsic (clarity, relevance, quality of evidence) or extrinsic (other framings, evidence or literature). "Acknowledging too many objections distracts from your core point, while acknowledging too few suggests carelessness." Admitting a question you cannot answer builds good faith. — [LessWrong notes](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/fvJECtgMX4cP62t2s/cliffnotes-to-craft-of-research-parts-i-ii-and-iii)
- A BU worksheet built on this model asks students to list four objections and answer the two "best" ones substantively. — [BU worksheet](https://www.bu.edu/teaching-writing/files/2020/04/Acknowledgement-and-Response-Student-In-Class-Activity-Option-2-worksheet.pdf)
- Hyland, "Stance and engagement: a model of interaction in academic discourse," Discourse Studies 7(2):173-192 (2005). Stance consists of hedges, boosters, attitude markers and self-mention. Engagement consists of reader references, directives, questions, appeals to shared knowledge and personal asides. Hedges present information as opinion, boosters signal certainty, and self-mentions are first-person pronouns and possessives. These summaries are secondary. — [U. Alberta WAC quick guide](https://ualberta.ca/en/centre-for-teaching-and-learning/media-library/wac/quick-guides/wacuofaframingyourresearchforreadersinteractionengagementstance.pdf); [Essex thesis, Menkabu 2017](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/19115/1/Menkabu%202017%20Stance%20and%20engagement%20in%20postgraduate%20writing.pdf)
- Mensh & Kording: one central contribution, an introduction that narrows to a specific gap without a broad literature review, results as a logical sequence of declarative statements, and a discussion that states how the gap was filled, the limitations of the interpretation, and the relevance to the field. Each weakness is linked to the literature. — [Mensh & Kording 2017](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619)

### Inferences (candidate skill text)
- **NEW. Contribution sentence.** Before drafting, write one sentence that a reader could repeat a year later: "We show that X, which matters because Y." It goes in the title or abstract and in the last paragraph of the introduction, and the discussion opens with it. Test: can a cold reader (commandment 1) state the contribution after reading the abstract alone? Fail: the abstract lists activities ("we examine, we analyze, we discuss") without a finding.
- **SHARPENS-4. Condition plus cost.** "Without this step, ..." (commandment 4) becomes: state the condition (what is not known or not possible) and the cost to the reader's field (what they cannot do or conclude because of it). Fail: the cost is the authors' effort ("this required significant engineering").
- **NEW. Synthesis, not catalog, in related work.** Each related-work paragraph starts with a claim about the literature (what a group of studies established or assumed, and where they stop) and ends by naming the gap this paper fills. Fail: a paragraph whose sentences each begin with a different author ("Smith et al. propose ... Jones et al. study ... Lee et al. introduce ..."). The topic-string test from section 1 will show it: if the topic position changes author in every sentence, the paragraph is a list. This inference combines Mensh & Kording Rule 6 with Gopen & Swan's topic position; no source here states it in these words.
- **SHARPENS-5. Which objections to answer.** The skill now says to answer only questions the reader has. Booth et al. add that a field reader will have some objections, and leaving those out reads as carelessness. Test: for every rebuttal sentence, name the reader who would raise it. "A reviewer at this venue" or "anyone who knows method M" passes. "Our internal reviewer" or "nobody" fails, so cut the sentence. Then list the two most plausible field-standard objections (alternative explanation, confound, threat to generalizability). If either is missing, add an acknowledgment and response at the point where the reader would raise it, or in Limitations.
- **NEW. Warrant check.** For each main claim, write the warrant: the general principle that makes the evidence count for the claim. If a reader in the field could reasonably reject the warrant, state it in the text. LLM drafts often place a fact next to a claim and leave the inference unstated.
- **NEW. Stance audit** (Hyland). Count self-mention commitments ("we argue", "we show", "we find"), boosters ("clearly", "demonstrates"), hedges and attitude markers in the abstract, introduction and discussion. Fail: no self-mention commitment in the introduction's contribution paragraph. Fail: hedges outnumber boosters on the paper's main claims while the evidence is strong. Fail: a booster on a claim the evidence does not size (commandment 12). Each main claim should carry exactly one epistemic marker that matches its evidence.
- **NEW. Falsifiable phrasing.** State each main claim so that a stated result would have contradicted it ("If X were the cause, we would expect Y; we observe not-Y"). Fail: claims that no observation could contradict ("X plays an important role in Y"). This is my inference. The sources consulted do not state it as a writing rule.

### Gaps
- I could not access Hyland's frequency data (for example markers per 1,000 words by discipline). The skill therefore cannot cite empirical norms for how much hedging is typical.
- I found no primary source on how strong papers word a limitation, beyond Mensh & Kording's "limitations of the interpretation" linked to the literature.

## 5. Which principles counter typical LLM failure modes

### Takeaway
Most of the professor's complaints map onto specific mechanical checks. List-of-facts paragraphs fail the topic-string, abstraction-level and promise tests. Generic topic sentences fail the level-4 opening test. A "this" without a referent fails the demonstrative check. Stress positions wasted on hedges and citations fail the stress audit. Nominalization-heavy prose fails the verb-list and density tests. Flat evenhandedness fails the stance audit and the contribution sentence. The "cutesy and clever" register is partly a stress-position abuse: a punchline in the stress position of a short sentence.

### Cited Findings
- The professor's critique: LLM drafts list facts without connecting them and answer unrevealed objections. A later note added "cutesy and clever". — [/Users/eric/.claude/skills/serious-academic/SKILL.md, "Origin"](file:///Users/eric/.claude/skills/serious-academic/SKILL.md)
- Gopen & Swan: a passage of individually well-formed sentences with new information in every topic position leaves readers without "a clear sense of where we have been or where we are going". — [Gopen & Swan 1990](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~swanson/papers/science-of-writing.pdf)
- Hayot: a paragraph that "hovers around level three" lacks substance. Opening with a grand, sweeping statement is the wrong move. — [Thesis Whisperer](https://thesiswhisperer.com/2019/02/13/the-uneven-u/)
- Pinker: metadiscourse and compulsive hedging come from self-consciousness and self-protection, not from the reader's needs. — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2012/11/exorcising-the-curse-of-knowledge)

### Inferences (failure mode → check, as a table for the skill)
| LLM failure mode | Check (source) | Pass/fail criterion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| List-of-facts paragraph | Topic-string list (Gopen & Swan; Williams). Abstraction map (Hayot). Promise test (Gopen & Swan) | Most topic positions are old information. At least one sentence at level 3-5 and one at 1-2. Every new stress-position item is picked up |

| Paragraph trails off on a detail | C-C-C close (Mensh & Kording). Level-5 close (Hayot) | The last sentence states what the paragraph established |
| "This"/"It"/"There is" with no referent | Writer's Diet category | Every sentence-initial demonstrative has a noun after it |
| Stress position wasted on a hedge, citation or "as discussed" | Stress audit (Gopen & Swan) | The final words of a sentence carry its new claim |
| Nominalization-heavy, be-verb prose | Verb list (Gopen & Swan). Density 5% or more (Sword). Characters and actions (Williams) | Main verbs name actions. Subjects name actors |
| Flat evenhandedness or survey tone | Contribution sentence (Mensh & Kording). Stance audit (Hyland) | One central claim with "we show". Markers match evidence |
| Related work as a catalog | Topic-string test applied to related work | Topic positions hold concepts or claims, not a new author name in each sentence |
| Unasked rebuttal / missing real objection | Acknowledgment and response (Booth et al.) | Each rebuttal names who would raise it. The top two field objections are present |
| Fact next to claim, inference unstated | Warrant check (Booth et al.). Logical-gap test (Gopen & Swan) | A contestable warrant is stated |
| Metadiscourse ("In this section we will", "It is worth noting") | Pinker | Zero, apart from one roadmap sentence in a long introduction |

| Hedged data | Britt/Durik 2008 | No hedge on a measured value |
